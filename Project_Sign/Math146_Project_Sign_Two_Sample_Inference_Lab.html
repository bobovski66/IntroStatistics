Statistical briefing

This activity divides the 102 dated entries with exact object counts into two separate periods:

  • Group 1: January 16 through July 4, 1947
  • Group 2: July 5, 1947 through January 11, 1948

The cutoff gives both groups at least 30 entries and also provides enough single-object and multiple-object reports for the two-proportion procedure.

Interpret carefully: These are archival report entries, not random samples from all aerial events. Your conclusions apply to the stated comparison model.

Objectives

  • Distinguish independent samples from paired samples.
  • Conduct a two-sample \(z\)-test comparing two means.
  • Construct a confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\).
  • Conduct a two-sample \(z\)-test comparing two proportions.
  • Explain how the numerical and categorical comparisons describe the same change differently.

Reference formulas

\[ \operatorname{SE}_{\bar x_1-\bar x_2} =\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}, \qquad z=\frac{(\bar x_1-\bar x_2)-0}{\operatorname{SE}} \] \[ (\bar x_1-\bar x_2)\pm z^*\operatorname{SE} \] \[ \hat p_{\mathrm{pool}}=\frac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2}, \qquad z=\frac{\hat p_1-\hat p_2} {\sqrt{\hat p_{\mathrm{pool}}(1-\hat p_{\mathrm{pool}}) \left(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}\right)}} \]

Part 1. Independent or paired?

The study design must be identified before a formula is chosen. These are separate report entries from two periods, not repeated measurements on the same entries.

Part 2. Two-sample test for mean object count

The mean comparison keeps the complete numerical object counts. Large counts therefore affect both the group means and their variability.

Test whether the earlier period has a larger mean number of objects reported.

\[ H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:\mu_1>\mu_2 \]

Use \(\alpha=0.05\). For this activity, use the supplied group standard deviations as \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\).

Group\(n\)MeanSupplied \(\sigma\)
1: through July 44.643
2: July 5 onward1.481

\[ \bar x_1-\bar x_2=2.697, \] \[ \operatorname{SE} =\sqrt{\frac{4.643^2}{31}+\frac{1.481^2}{71}} =0.852, \] \[ z=\frac{2.697}{0.852}=3.164, \qquad p=P(Z\ge 3.164)=0.0008. \] Since \(p<0.05\), reject \(H_0\).

Part 3. Confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\)

The confidence interval estimates the size and direction of the difference, not merely whether a test crosses a significance threshold.

Use the same difference and standard error to construct a 95% confidence interval.

\[ \operatorname{ME}=1.96(0.852)=1.670, \] \[ 95\%\text{ CI for }\mu_1-\mu_2=(1.026,4.367). \] The interval is entirely positive and does not contain \(0\).

Does the interval contain 0?

Part 4. Two-sample test for proportions

The counts are now recoded as exactly one object or more than one. This second view is less sensitive to the size of the largest counts and describes the shift categorically.

Call an entry a success when it reports exactly one object. Test whether the proportions differ between the two periods.

\[ H_0:p_1=p_2 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:p_1\ne p_2 \]
GroupOne objectMore than oneTotal

All four counts are at least 10, so the normal approximation condition is satisfied.

\[ \hat p_1=\frac{14}{31}=0.4516, \qquad \hat p_2=\frac{56}{71}=0.7887, \] \[ \hat p_{\mathrm{pool}} =\frac{14+56}{31+71} =0.6863, \] \[ z=\frac{-0.3371}{0.0999}=-3.375, \qquad p=0.0007. \] Since \(p<0.05\), reject \(H_0\).

Part 5. Put the two results together

The mean and proportion procedures are different lenses on the same archive. Agreement between them strengthens the description of a shift, but neither procedure identifies its cause.
