Start here · The question

Can two groups have the same mean and still produce different samples?

Imagine a school comparing two teaching methods. Two groups of students take the same assessment. Their averages will almost never be exactly equal, even if the methods lead to the same population mean. Different samples contain different students, and students don't all earn the same score.

Last week, you tested whether one sample was unusually far from a claimed population mean. This week, we ask whether the difference between two sample means is unusually far from zero. Start with the Equal means preset on the left. Then draw a pair of samples. We'll walk through what the result means.

Step 1 · State the hypotheses

What would we expect if the groups were equal?

Our starting claim is that the two population means are equal. That is the null hypothesis. The alternative says the means are different. Since we're interested in differences in either direction, this is a two-tailed test.

Null hypothesisH₀: μ₁ − μ₂ = 0

In the populations, there is no difference in average scores.

Alternative hypothesisHₐ: μ₁ − μ₂ ≠ 0

The population mean scores differ in either direction.

The simulation knows the population means because we chose them. Imagine that we're researchers who don't know the truth: we must make our decision using the sampled students.

Step 2 · Calculate the test statistic

How far apart are the two sample means?

Every dot below is one student's simulated score. The diamond marks the average of the group. When you draw new samples, the dots and diamonds move. Even when both populations average 80 points, the two sample means will usually be different.

The observed difference is Group 1's sample mean minus Group 2's. But a difference alone doesn't tell us whether it is unusual. We compare it with the standard error, which measures how much we expect the difference to vary across repeated samples.

Draw two samples to begin.
Why do both groups contribute to the standard error?

Each sample mean varies. Taking their difference combines those two sources of uncertainty. For independent samples, the variances add, even though the means are subtracted:

SE = √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂)

This is the only new part of our familiar Week 6 z-statistic. The test still uses (observed − expected) ÷ standard error. Under H₀, the expected difference is zero.

Step 3 · Find the p-value

Would a difference this large be surprising under H₀?

This curve shows the differences we might see across repeated studies if the population means were equal. The center is zero. Differences close to zero are common; differences far from zero are less common.

Once you've drawn a sample, the darker shaded areas count differences at least as extreme as yours, in either direction. Those two tail areas make up the p-value. A small p-value tells us the observed difference would be unusual under the equality claim.

After drawing samples, you'll see the p-value explained here.
What are the pale regions, and why can they move?

The pale regions mark the rejection cutoffs for our chosen significance level, α. With the default 95% confidence setting, α = 0.05. The p-value is determined by the observed sample; α is the cutoff we chose for making a decision. Changing α changes the cutoff, not the p-value.

Step 4 · Make a conclusion

What can we reasonably say about the groups?

Just as in Week 6, compare the p-value with α. If the p-value is small enough, we reject the claim of equal population means. Otherwise, we fail to reject it. Failing to reject does not establish that the population means are equal.
