Remember: expected counts come from the claim. Observed counts come from the sample. Chi-squared asks how well they match.

Start with a question

When should we doubt a predicted pattern?

Imagine a cafeteria that believes three lunch choices are equally popular. It surveys 60 students. If the claim is right, it should expect about 20 choices for each lunch, but it would be surprising if every random sample matched that prediction perfectly.

We'll take the sample counts seriously without assuming every disagreement proves the cafeteria wrong. We need to measure how much the observations disagree with the model. That's precisely what the chi-squared test is designed to do.

Step 1 · What does the claim predict?

Compare expected counts with observed counts

Start with the claim that the categories are equally likely. With three lunches and 60 students, that predicts 20 responses in each category. The table updates as you move the sliders on the left.

Expected counts are the predictions of the model; observed counts are what we actually recorded.

The sign of O − E tells us whether the sample showed more or fewer observations than the model predicted. Positive and negative gaps can cancel if we merely add them, so the test needs another way to combine those differences.

Step 2 · Measure the total mismatch

Give each category a contribution, then add them

For each category, square the gap between observed and expected counts, then divide by the expected count. Squaring keeps positive and negative gaps from cancelling. Dividing by the expected count makes a gap of five count more when we expected ten than when we expected a hundred.

Chi-squared: χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E

Why do we have degrees of freedom?

All the counts together add to 60. Once the counts for the first two lunches are chosen, the third one is fixed. That's why a goodness-of-fit test with three categories has two degrees of freedom. More generally, for a fixed set of k claimed category probabilities, the degrees of freedom are df = k − 1.

A larger χ² means a bigger overall mismatch. Every contribution is nonnegative, so the statistic can never be negative. A perfect match gives χ² = 0.

Step 3 · Find the p-value

How unusual is this mismatch if the claim is true?

Imagine repeating the survey many times under the cafeteria's equal-choice model. Some samples would be close to the prediction; others would be farther away. The chi-squared curve tells us how unusual a given total mismatch would be under the null hypothesis.

Because larger χ² values mean more disagreement, we always look in the right tail of this curve. The shaded area to the right of our observed statistic is the p-value.

Step 4 · Conclude in context

Does the sample make us doubt the model?

Compare the p-value with α, just as in Weeks 6 and 7. A small enough p-value means that this pattern of counts is unusual under the model, so we reject it. Otherwise, we fail to reject. We never prove a model true merely because the sample is compatible with it.

Optional · The geometry behind categorical models

There is a geometric picture behind the counts, though you do not need it to carry out the test. If the categories have probabilities that add to one, those possible models form a line for two categories, a triangle for three, and a tetrahedron for four. Our predicted model is one point; the observed proportions give another.

Optional · Repeat the survey under a true null model

In a real investigation, we do not know whether our null model is true. Here we do: the computer generates new surveys from the exact probabilities we have chosen. You can see that even with a correct model, some random samples give substantial mismatches.
