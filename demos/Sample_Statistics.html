Week 1 · Describing a sample

Shape, Center, and Spread

A list of numbers is difficult to reason about all at once. This page helps you turn one quantitative sample into a picture and a small set of summaries, then asks the more important question: what do those summaries tell you about the sample?

Driving question: How can a histogram and a few carefully chosen statistics tell the story of a quantitative sample without hiding important features of the data?
1 · Name the variableKnow what one observation represents and what units are being measured.
2 · See the distributionLook for clusters, gaps, tails, and unusual values before focusing on formulas.
3 · SummarizeUse center and spread to compress the sample into interpretable numbers.
4 · InterpretTranslate the graph and statistics back into a statement about the sample.

Before you enter data: give the numbers meaning

A value such as 42 means very little by itself. It might be 42 minutes, 42 sightings, 42 centimeters, or 42 points. Statistical summaries become useful only after the variable and its units are clear.

Statistical boundary: everything on this page describes the sample you enter. A sample mean or histogram does not automatically describe a larger population, explain why the pattern occurred, or establish cause and effect.
Step 1

Enter one quantitative variable

If a CSV contains several columns, you will choose the quantitative column after the file is read.

Why one variable? A histogram is a picture of the distribution of one quantitative variable. Later in the course, you will compare two groups or study relationships between two variables; here the goal is to learn how to read one distribution well.
Step 2

Choose how the distribution is displayed

Histogram or exact-value chart? Use a histogram when many quantitative values are spread across an interval. Grouping values into classes makes the overall shape easier to see. Use exact values when there are only a few possible outcomes, such as scores from 0 through 10.
Try moving the number-of-classes slider after the histogram appears. The sample does not change, but the picture can. A histogram is a useful summary, not a photograph of the data.

Read shape before center

Look for one or more clusters, gaps, long tails, and observations separated from the rest. A mean alone cannot show any of these.

Then compare mean and median

If the mean and median are close, they give similar descriptions of center. A noticeable difference is a reason to inspect the histogram for skew or unusual values.

Turn the output back into statistics

A useful final sentence has context: “In this sample of …, the typical value was about …, with … spread, and the distribution appeared … .” Use the histogram to finish the shape description rather than letting the computer choose the words for you.
Why the computer does not label the shape for you: shape is partly a judgment based on the graph and the purpose of the analysis. Automated labels such as “skewed” can be convenient, but learning to see the evidence yourself is more valuable at this stage.
