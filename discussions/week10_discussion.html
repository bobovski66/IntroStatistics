Case file

The problem

When investigators find a sequence of footprints, they often measure more than the length of each print. Another useful measurement is stride length: roughly, the distance from one footprint to the next corresponding footprint in the trackway. For many ordinary animals, body size, leg length, speed, terrain, and gait all influence stride, so we might expect footprint length and stride length to be related without expecting one variable to determine the other perfectly.

Suppose a field team wants to explore whether longer footprints tend to occur with longer strides. To practice the statistical method without pretending that these numbers establish the existence of any unusual animal, we will use the following fabricated trackway dataset. Here x is footprint length in centimeters and y is stride length in centimeters.

A scatterplot lets us see whether a roughly linear pattern is plausible. If it is, a least-squares regression line summarizes the average relationship between the two variables. The fitted line can then be used for prediction within the range of the observed data, while residuals tell us how far individual observations fall above or below those predictions.

Regression can describe association very effectively, but it has limits. A strong correlation does not establish a causal mechanism, and a line fitted to footprints between 28 cm and 40 cm has not been tested on tracks far outside that range.

The statistical question: How well does a linear model summarize the relationship between footprint length and stride length in this dataset, and what would be unsafe to conclude or predict from that model?

Initial post

What to include

Use the Regression Explorer with the trackway preset, or the calculator. Keep the original seven fabricated pairs for your reported fit. Build your post from picture to prediction to model check.

  1. Make a scatterplot and describe the direction, form, and strength of the association.
  2. Find the least-squares line and correlation r. Explain why its line passes through the two sample means, rather than just quoting the formulas.
  3. Use the fitted line to predict stride length for a 35 cm footprint.
  4. For the observation x = 34, y = 132, compute the residual as observed minus predicted.
  5. Interpret r² in context. Then explain why even a very strong linear association would not prove that footprint length alone determines stride length, and why extrapolating far beyond 28–40 cm would be risky. Connect one limitation to the course’s earlier work on sampling, influential observations, or the distinction between association and cause.
