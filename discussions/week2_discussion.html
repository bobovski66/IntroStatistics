Week 2 Discussion — Waiting for the Light

Use geometric and binomial models to describe a night-watch protocol.

How this discussion works

Solve, present, then respond

Work the problem below before reading classmates’ solutions. In your initial post, show enough statistical work that another student can follow your method, then state the result in the language of the situation. Post your solution in the class discussion board; this page itself does not submit responses.

A clear table, calculation, labeled graph, calculator output, or photo of neatly organized handwritten work is fine when appropriate. The goal is statistical communication, not extra prose.

Case file

The problem

A skywatch group defines a qualified anomalous-light event using a fixed checklist: the light must persist for at least 10 seconds, move against the apparent star field, and be recorded by two cameras. The label is operational; it does not say what caused the light.

Under a simplified probability model, suppose each 10-minute observation window has probability p = 0.18 of containing a qualified event, and treat different windows as independent.

The group watches eight consecutive windows. One question concerns when the first qualified event occurs; another concerns how many qualified events occur in all eight windows. Those are different random variables.

Initial post

What to include

  1. Using a geometric model, find the probability that the first qualified event occurs in the fourth observation window.
  2. Find the expected number of windows the group must watch before the first qualified event occurs.
  3. Using a binomial model with n = 8 and p = 0.18, find the probability of exactly two qualified events.
  4. Find the expected number of qualified events in eight windows.
  5. Explain, in words, why the geometric and binomial questions use the same value of p but model different things. Also identify one assumption in the simplified model that could fail in a real skywatch.
Replies

Engage with classmates’ statistics

Choose a classmate who organized the two models differently from you. Verify one probability, then explain whether their description of the random variable makes it clear why the geometric and binomial models are not interchangeable.

A useful reply checks a calculation, compares methods or assumptions, or asks a statistically specific question. Avoid replies that only say that you agree.
