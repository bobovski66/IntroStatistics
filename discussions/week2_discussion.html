Week 2 Discussion — Waiting for the Light

Use geometric and binomial models to describe a night-watch protocol.

How this discussion works

Solve, present, then respond

Work the problem below before reading classmates’ solutions. In your initial post, show enough statistical work that another student can follow your method, then state the result in the language of the situation. Post your solution in the class discussion board; this page itself does not submit responses.

A clear table, calculation, labeled graph, calculator output, or photo of neatly organized handwritten work is fine when appropriate. The goal is statistical communication, not extra prose.

Case file

The problem

Imagine a small skywatch group that spends several nights monitoring the same patch of sky with two synchronized cameras. Because people can disagree about what counts as “something strange,” the group decides in advance to use a fixed rule. A light is recorded as a qualified anomalous-light event only if it lasts at least 10 seconds, appears to move against the apparent star field, and is captured by both cameras.

This definition is intentionally operational: it tells the observers how to classify what they see, but it does not identify the object or explain its cause. A qualified event could eventually turn out to be an aircraft, satellite, atmospheric effect, unknown object, or something else. For the probability model, all that matters is whether an observation window satisfies the rule.

The group divides the night into consecutive 10-minute observation windows. From earlier nights they build a simplified model in which each window has probability p = 0.18 of containing at least one qualified event. For this exercise, also assume that one window does not affect the next, so the windows may be treated as independent.

Now suppose the group watches eight consecutive windows. There are two natural questions they might ask. One is, “How long will we wait until the first qualified event?” The other is, “How many qualified events will occur in the eight windows altogether?” Those questions sound similar, but they define different random variables and therefore lead to different probability models.

The statistical question: When should we use a geometric model, when should we use a binomial model, and what assumptions are we quietly making when we apply either one to a real observing session?

Initial post

What to include

  1. Using a geometric model, find the probability that the first qualified event occurs in the fourth observation window.
  2. Find the expected number of windows the group must watch before the first qualified event occurs.
  3. Using a binomial model with n = 8 and p = 0.18, find the probability of exactly two qualified events.
  4. Find the expected number of qualified events in eight windows.
  5. Explain, in words, why the geometric and binomial questions use the same value of p but model different things. Also identify one assumption in the simplified model that could fail in a real skywatch.
Replies

Engage with classmates’ statistics

Choose a classmate who organized the two models differently from you. Verify one probability, then explain whether their description of the random variable makes it clear why the geometric and binomial models are not interchangeable.

A useful reply checks a calculation, compares methods or assumptions, or asks a statistically specific question. Avoid replies that only say that you agree.
