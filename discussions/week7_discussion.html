Case file

The problem

Researchers sometimes use automated acoustic systems to scan long recordings for sounds that deserve closer inspection. Imagine a wildlife-acoustics team looking for low-frequency vocalizations in two forest regions. Their software uses the same fixed detection rule in both places and flags sounds that do not match any source already represented in its reference library.

A flagged sound is therefore called an unidentified source only in the technical sense that the algorithm did not classify it. It may eventually prove to be a known animal, machinery, wind, a recording artifact, or something genuinely unfamiliar. The statistical analysis begins with a simpler variable: the duration, in seconds, of each flagged vocalization.

The team collects separate recordings in the Olympic foothills and the Cascade foothills. The two sets come from different detectors on different nights, so there is no natural one-to-one match between an Olympic observation and a Cascade observation.

Olympic foothills
n₁ = 50
x̄₁ = 8.4 s
s₁ = 3.0 s
Cascade foothills
n₂ = 45
x̄₂ = 7.1 s
s₂ = 2.8 s

That design makes these independent samples. The distinction matters because two-sample statistics use a different standard error from a paired design, where each observation in one condition is naturally linked to a specific observation in the other.

The statistical question: What does the sample evidence suggest about the difference in mean flagged-sound duration between the two regions, and why must we identify the study design before choosing the calculation?

Initial post

What to include

  1. Explain why these samples are independent rather than paired.
  2. Compute x̄₁ − x̄₂.
  3. Compute SE(x̄₁−x̄₂) = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂).
  4. Using 1.96 as an approximate 95% multiplier, construct an interval for μ₁ − μ₂ and interpret it.
  5. Suppose instead that the same 40 detectors were measured before and after a software update. Explain why that new design would be paired and what information the pairing preserves.
