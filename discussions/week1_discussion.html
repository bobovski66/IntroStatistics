Week 1 Discussion — The Sasquatch Trackway

Describe a strange sample without letting one dramatic observation tell the whole story.

How this discussion works

Solve, present, then respond

Work the problem below before reading classmates’ solutions. In your initial post, show enough statistical work that another student can follow your method, then state the result in the language of the situation. Post your solution in the class discussion board; this page itself does not submit responses.

A clear table, calculation, labeled graph, calculator output, or photo of neatly organized handwritten work is fine when appropriate. The goal is statistical communication, not extra prose.

Case file

The problem

Suppose a field team is surveying a remote logging road after several hikers report seeing unusually large footprints in the area. The team is not beginning with the assumption that the tracks came from a Sasquatch; at this stage, the investigators simply have a collection of physical impressions that can be measured and compared. Their first job is the same job a statistician often faces: describe what was actually observed before trying to explain it.

The team finds 12 impressions clear enough to cast and measure. Their footprint lengths, in centimeters, are

31, 32, 32, 33, 34, 34, 35, 35, 36, 37, 38, 52

For every print, the investigators also record other information, including the substrate in which it was found (mud, sand, gravel, or leaf litter), its GPS region, and whether the impression was complete enough to preserve. Notice that some of these measurements are numerical while others describe categories. That distinction determines what kinds of summaries make sense.

The team would eventually like to say something about the larger collection of castable impressions that might be found in the region during the season, but they have only these 12 observations in hand. One footprint, at 52 cm, is much longer than the others. It may be a perfectly genuine observation, a measurement problem, a print from a different source, or simply an unusually large value. The data alone do not yet tell us which.

The statistical question: How should we describe this sample fairly, and how much does one unusually large observation change the numerical story told by the data?

Read the records first

What does one observation represent?

One case is one measurable impression, rather than one identified animal. Repeated lengths still count as separate impressions. Several impressions could have come from the same source, so twelve observations need not be twelve independent animals.

The sample includes impressions that were found and clear enough to cast. Hidden, damaged, inaccessible, or uncastable impressions have no representation here. Consider that selection process when describing what this sample can tell us about the region during the season.

Make a dot plot or use the one above, label centimeters, and keep the original twelve values visible. Predict the effect of the 52 cm impression before comparing numerical summaries.

Initial post

What to include

  1. State the population the team would like to learn about and identify the sample actually observed.
  2. Classify footprint length and substrate type as quantitative or categorical variables.
  3. Find the mean and median footprint length for the 12-print sample.
  4. Temporarily set aside the 52 cm impression and recompute both the mean and median. Report the change in each, with units. Which summary is more affected? Restore the full sample after this sensitivity comparison.
  5. Would it be statistically responsible to delete the 52 cm print merely because it is unusual? Explain what you would want to check before deciding how to treat it.
