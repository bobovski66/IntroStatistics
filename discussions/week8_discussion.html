Week 8 Discussion — The Cryptid Hotline Mix

Use a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test to decide whether a new report mix matches an old model.

How this discussion works

Work the problem below before reading classmates’ solutions. In your initial post, show enough statistical work that another student can follow your method, then state the result in the language of the situation. Post your solution in the class discussion board; this page itself does not submit responses.

A clear table, calculation, labeled graph, calculator output, or photo of neatly organized handwritten work is fine when appropriate. The goal is statistical communication, not extra prose.

Case file

The problem

Imagine a regional hotline that has collected unusual-animal reports for many years. To make the archive searchable, each incoming report is placed into one of several broad descriptive categories. The categories are based on what the caller describes, not on independent confirmation that the reported creature exists.

Over a long historical period, the hotline’s reports have settled into a fairly stable pattern: about 50% are classified as large-footed hominid reports, 30% as winged-humanoid reports, and 20% as lake- or river-creature reports. We can treat those percentages as a probability model for the distribution of report categories.

Large-footed hominid
50%
Winged humanoid
30%
Lake or river creature
20%

During a new reporting period, the hotline receives 200 reports. The observed counts are 120, 50, and 30 in those same categories. If the historical percentages still describe the reporting process well, we would not expect the observed counts to match the expected counts exactly; random variation creates some difference every time.

A chi-squared goodness-of-fit test asks whether the observed differences are modest enough to be explained by ordinary random variation or large enough to make the historical category model doubtful. The test pays attention not only to the total discrepancy but to how large each discrepancy is relative to its expected count.

The statistical question: Does the new mix of report categories fit the historical 50%–30%–20% model reasonably well, and which category is contributing most strongly to any mismatch?

Initial post

What to include

  1. Compute the expected counts under the historical model.
  2. Compute each category’s chi-squared contribution (O−E)²/E and add them to obtain χ².
  3. Find the degrees of freedom.
  4. Use a chi-squared calculator or table to estimate the p-value and decide whether the new report mix is consistent with the historical model at α = 0.05.
  5. Identify which category contributes most to χ². Explain why a change in the mix of reports is not automatically evidence that the underlying creatures became more or less common.
Replies

Engage with classmates’ statistics

Check one classmate’s expected count and one chi-squared contribution. Then compare which category each of you sees as driving the discrepancy and whether your interpretations stay focused on the report distribution rather than the reality of the reported creatures.

A useful reply checks a calculation, compares methods or assumptions, or asks a statistically specific question. Avoid replies that only say that you agree.
