Week 8 Discussion — Does the Hotline's Report Pattern Still Fit?

Use observed and expected category counts to carry out a familiar hypothesis test with chi-squared.

What we're practicing

A hypothesis test about a whole pattern

Weeks 6 and 7 taught us to compare sample results with what a null hypothesis predicts. This week you will use that same reasoning when the data are counts in categories. First understand the model; then predict counts, measure the total mismatch with chi-squared, and decide whether the sample gives us evidence against the model.

In your initial post, explain your reasoning along with the arithmetic. You can show a table, a graph, calculator work, or clearly organized handwritten calculations. Post your work to the class discussion board; this page is a prompt, not a submission form.

Case file

A change in the pattern of reports?

Imagine a regional hotline that has spent many years collecting reports of unusual animals. The volunteers running the hotline want to organize those reports so they can be searched and compared from year to year. Each report is assigned to a descriptive category based on what the caller says—not on independent evidence that the animal exists.

Over time, the volunteers notice that the mix of reports is surprisingly steady. Some kinds of descriptions are common, and others come in much less frequently. They begin to wonder whether a new reporting period looks similar to the historical pattern. If the new mix is quite different, perhaps something has changed about where calls are coming from, which stories get attention, or how the hotline reaches people.

This question is about the pattern of reports, not the existence or population sizes of the creatures described. And it is exactly the sort of question a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test can help answer.

Before the new reporting period begins, suppose the hotline describes the historical mix with three percentages: 50% large-footed hominid reports, 30% winged-humanoid reports, and 20% lake- or river-creature reports. Think of those percentages as the null model: the prediction we will compare the new observations with.

Large-footed hominid reports
Model probability: 50%
Winged-humanoid reports
Model probability: 30%
Lake- or river-creature reports
Model probability: 20%

Now the volunteers count 200 new reports. Of these, 120 fall in the first category, 50 in the second, and 30 in the third. If the historical model were still correct, these new observations would not necessarily match its predictions perfectly. We need to decide whether the disagreements are substantial enough to doubt that model.

We will use a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test at α = 0.05. Ask yourself what the null predicts before you work with the formula. How many of the 200 new reports would we expect in each category if the percentages remained 50%–30%–20%?

Initial post

Explain the pattern, then carry out the test

Organize your initial post around the same steps we have used for hypothesis testing. Answer every step and show the calculations that support your conclusion.

  1. Step 1 — State. In your own words, what does the null hypothesis say about the percentages of reports in each category? What does the alternative say? Remember that the alternative concerns the overall distribution.
  2. Step 2 — Expected counts. If the model is correct, how many of the 200 reports should belong to each of the three categories on average? Show how you translated the percentages into expected counts.
  3. Step 2 — Chi-squared. Compute \((O-E)^2/E\) for each category, then add the three contributions to find \(\chi^2\). Which category contributes the most, and is its observed count above or below its expected count?
  4. Step 3 — p-value. Find the degrees of freedom and use a chi-squared table or calculator to judge the right-tail probability. For three categories, the 5% critical value is 5.991. Does the observed statistic fall in the rejection region?
  5. Step 4 — Conclude. At \(\alpha=0.05\), what do these reports suggest about whether the reporting pattern still fits its historical model? What would be an inappropriate conclusion about the actual creatures?
Replies

Help classmates interpret the evidence

Choose a classmate's work and check one expected count and one chi-squared contribution. Then respond to their interpretation: did they describe a change in the mix of reported categories, or claim more than the data establish?

A useful reply gives a concrete statistical check or explains how one contribution affects the overall statistic.
