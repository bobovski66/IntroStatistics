Case file

The problem

Point Pleasant, West Virginia, is famously associated with reports of a large winged figure sometimes called the Mothman. Imagine that, for research purposes, a local hotline has kept nightly counts of calls reporting a “large winged figure” for many years. We are not going to treat every call as a confirmed creature sighting. The variable being measured is simply the number of reports received each night.

Historical records show that nightly report counts are strongly right-skewed: most nights are fairly quiet, while a few nights produce bursts of calls. Across the long-run archive, the population mean is μ = 4.8 calls per night and the population standard deviation is σ = 6.0 calls. Because the individual nights are so variable, seeing one night with a fairly large count is not automatically remarkable.

Now suppose that during a 36-night festival season the hotline receives an average of 7.1 calls per night. This is a different kind of observation. Instead of asking about one night, we are asking about the average of many nights. Averages fluctuate less than individual observations, and the amount of that fluctuation is measured by the standard error.

For this exercise, assume the 36 nightly counts are independent enough for the Central Limit Theorem to be useful. Even though the individual nightly counts are not normally distributed, the CLT tells us that the distribution of averages from many 36-night samples can be approximately normal.

The statistical question: Is an average of 7.1 calls over 36 nights unusually high under the historical reporting pattern, and why can a sample average be statistically surprising even when individual nights are noisy?

Initial post

What to include

  1. Find the standard error of the mean for samples of 36 nights.
  2. Compute the z-score of the observed sample mean 7.1 under the historical model.
  3. Use the normal model for sample means to estimate P(x̄ ≥ 7.1).
  4. An individual night with 11 calls has z = (11 − 4.8)/6. Explain why an average of 7.1 over 36 nights can be more statistically unusual than one 11-call night.
  5. Explain why the Central Limit Theorem can make the sampling distribution of x̄ approximately normal even though the individual nightly counts are right-skewed.
