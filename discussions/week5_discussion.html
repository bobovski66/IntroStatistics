Week 5 Discussion — How Many Frames Stay Unexplained?

Build a confidence interval around a proportion without turning uncertainty into a supernatural conclusion.

How this discussion works

Solve, present, then respond

Work the problem below before reading classmates’ solutions. In your initial post, show enough statistical work that another student can follow your method, then state the result in the language of the situation. Post your solution in the class discussion board; this page itself does not submit responses.

A clear table, calculation, labeled graph, calculator output, or photo of neatly organized handwritten work is fine when appropriate. The goal is statistical communication, not extra prose.

Case file

The problem

Trail cameras generate enormous numbers of nighttime photographs, and many perfectly ordinary images can initially look mysterious. An insect close to the lens may become a bright blur; fog can reflect infrared light; an animal can be only partly visible; motion can produce smearing. Suppose a research group wants to know how often images remain unidentified after a standard review process rather than relying on first impressions.

The group has an archive containing thousands of nighttime frames from comparable forest sites. Reviewing every image would be impractical, so the investigators randomly select 200 frames. Each frame is examined using the same checklist. Images that can be classified as known animals, people, insects, lens flare, camera artifacts, or other ordinary sources are removed from the “unexplained” category.

After the review, 34 of the 200 frames remain labeled unexplained after review. That label is important: it means only that the team could not confidently identify the source using the available information. It does not mean that 34 photographs show a cryptid.

Because these 200 frames are only a sample, the observed proportion 34/200 will not be exactly equal to the proportion we would obtain if every comparable image in the archive were reviewed. A confidence interval gives us a reasonable range of population proportions compatible with the sample.

The statistical question: What does this random sample suggest about the proportion of comparable archived images that would remain unexplained under the same review procedure, and how should we communicate the uncertainty in that estimate?

Initial post

What to include

  1. Compute the sample proportion p̂.
  2. Compute the standard error SE = √(p̂(1−p̂)/n).
  3. Construct an approximate 95% confidence interval using p̂ ± 1.96(SE).
  4. Interpret the interval in the language of the archive and review procedure.
  5. Explain what the confidence interval does not tell you about the cause of the unexplained frames.
Replies

Engage with classmates’ statistics

Check a classmate’s interval endpoints. Then compare interpretations: does the wording correctly describe uncertainty about a population proportion, or does it accidentally claim more about the unexplained images than the data establish?

A useful reply checks a calculation, compares methods or assumptions, or asks a statistically specific question. Avoid replies that only say that you agree.
