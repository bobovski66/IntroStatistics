Normal Models, Sampling, and the Central Limit Theorem

An average is itself a random result when its sample changes. Understanding that variation is the bridge from description to inference.

How to use this reading

  • Calculate probabilities and cutoffs for general normal models.
  • Distinguish data distributions from sampling distributions.

1. Move and rescale the normal curve

A normal model is determined by its mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). This course writes it as \(N(\mu,\sigma)\): the second entry is the standard deviation. Other texts use variance instead, so always check the convention. Changing the mean shifts the curve; increasing the standard deviation widens it while preserving total area 1.

\[z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\qquad x=\mu+z\sigma\]
Worked example.

Heights are modeled as normal with mean 67 inches and standard deviation 3 inches. For 61 inches, \(z=(61-67)/3=-2\). The model predicts \(P(X\le61)=\Phi(-2)=0.0228\).

For an interval, standardize both endpoints and subtract their left-tail areas. For a percentile, find the corresponding z-value first, then convert back to the original units.

Check your understanding.

Exam scores follow a normal model with mean 72 and standard deviation 8. Find the cutoff for the top 10%.

Show explanation

The 90th-percentile z-value is about 1.282. The score is \(72+1.282(8)\approx82.26\).

2. Three distributions that answer different questions

The population distribution describes individual values across the population. A sample distribution describes the values in one collected sample. A sampling distribution describes a statistic across repeated samples of the same size.

Imagine selecting 25 students, computing their mean height, returning to the same population, and repeating the process. Each sample produces one mean. A histogram of those means is a sampling distribution, not a histogram of individual heights.

DistributionOne plotted value represents
Population or sample dataOne individual observation
Sampling distribution of the meanOne entire sample’s average
Check your understanding.

A simulation draws 1,000 samples of size 40 and plots their means. How many values are plotted?

Show explanation

1,000 means, one for each sample, rather than all 40,000 individual observations.

3. Standard error measures variation among averages

For independent observations from a population with mean \(\mu\) and finite standard deviation \(\sigma\), the sample mean has mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). We call this standard deviation the standard error. These center and spread formulas do not require the population to be normal.

\[E(\bar X)=\mu,\qquad SE(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]
Worked example.

For heights with \(\sigma=3\) inches, samples of size 9 have standard error 1 inch; samples of size 36 have standard error 0.5 inch. The population spread stays 3 inches.

Independent sampling matters. Measurements clustered within families or repeated over time can be dependent. For sampling without replacement, treating observations as approximately independent is generally reasonable when the sample is a small fraction of the population; the common guideline is no more than 10%. Larger fractions require a finite-population adjustment.

Check your understanding.

How much must n increase to halve standard error?

Show explanation

Multiply n by 4, because its square root then doubles.

4. What the CLT does, and what it does not do

For independent, identically distributed observations with finite variance, the Central Limit Theorem says that the standardized sample mean approaches a standard normal distribution as sample size grows. This often makes sample means approximately normal even when individual measurements are skewed.

\[\bar X\approx N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right)\]

If the population is normal, the sample mean is exactly normal at every sample size under independent sampling. Otherwise, the necessary sample size depends on skewness and tails. A sample size of 30 is a useful classroom guideline, not a universal guarantee; strong skewness or rare extreme values can require much larger samples.

The CLT does not make the individual observations normal and does not repair biased selection. A large sample from a skewed population can still have a skewed histogram of its raw observations.

Check your understanding.

A sample contains 100 skewed waiting times. Must its data histogram be bell-shaped?

Show explanation

No. The CLT concerns the distribution of averages over repeated samples, not the distribution of the individual waiting times.

5. Calculate a probability for an average

First identify whether the question concerns one observation or a sample mean. For an individual, standardize with σ; for a mean, standardize with the standard error.

\[z=\frac{\bar x-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\]
Worked example.

Cup fills are independent and normal with mean 8.1 ounces and standard deviation 0.4 ounces. For n = 16, standard error is \(0.4/4=0.10\). The endpoints 7.95 and 8.25 become z = −1.5 and 1.5. Thus \(P(7.95\le\bar X\le8.25)=0.9332-0.0668=0.8664\).

This probability describes repeated samples of 16 cups. For one cup, the same interval has endpoints z = ±0.375 and probability about 0.2923. Averages cluster much more tightly than individual values.

Looking ahead.

A confidence interval turns this description of sampling variation into a range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter.
