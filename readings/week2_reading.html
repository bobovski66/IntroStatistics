1. From observed frequencies to a probability model

Last week, relative frequencies described a collected sample. A probability distribution describes the possible results of a random process. A random variable, usually written X, assigns a numerical value to each result; lowercase x denotes a particular value.

For discrete X, a probability mass function (PMF) assigns P(X = x) to each possible value. Every probability is nonnegative, and all probabilities sum to 1. To find the probability of several mutually exclusive outcomes, add their probabilities.

Worked example.
x012
P(X = x)0.200.500.30

These probabilities total 1. The probability of at least one is 0.50 + 0.30 = 0.80.

On a probability bar chart, bar heights represent probability masses. They sum to 1; their areas also do so when the bars have unit width. This differs from a continuous density curve, where probability is area rather than height.

Can probabilities 0.2, 0.4, 0.5 define a complete distribution?

Show explanation

No. Their sum is 1.1, exceeding the total probability available.

2. Expected value is a weighted average

The expected value is the model’s long-run average. Multiply each numerical outcome by its probability and add. Likely outcomes carry more weight. An expected value need not be a possible individual outcome.

\[\mu=E(X)=\sum_x xP(X=x)\]
Worked example.

Using the table above, \(E(X)=0(0.20)+1(0.50)+2(0.30)=1.10\). Individual results are 0, 1, or 2, but the long-run average can approach 1.10.

In a game, define the random variable carefully. If a game costs $2 and pays $10 only on a die roll of 6, the net outcomes are $8 and −$2, not $10 and $0.

\[E(\text{net gain})=8\left(\frac16\right)-2\left(\frac56\right)=-\frac13\text{ dollar}\]

The model predicts an average loss of about 33 cents per play over many plays. The law of large numbers explains why averages stabilize around the expectation under suitable independent repetition; it does not promise a particular short sequence.

3. Variance weights squared deviations by probability

The theoretical variance averages squared distances from the expected value using probability weights. There is no n−1 adjustment here: the distribution itself is given rather than estimated by fitting a sample mean.

\[\sigma^2=\sum_x(x-\mu)^2P(X=x),\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\]
Worked example.

With \(\mu=1.10\), variance is \((0-1.10)^2(0.20)+(1-1.10)^2(0.50)+(2-1.10)^2(0.30)=0.49\). Therefore \(\sigma=0.70\).

Check your understanding.

Two games have the same expected gain but different standard deviations. Are they equivalent?

Show explanation

They have the same long-run average gain, but different variability. The game with greater standard deviation has outcomes more spread around its expectation.

4. Geometric: wait until the first success

A geometric model counts trials up to and including the first success. Trials must be independent, have two outcomes, and keep the same success probability p. “Success” means the outcome being counted, even if it is something undesirable such as a defective part. Here p is greater than 0 and at most 1.

\[P(X=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\quad k=1,2,\ldots;\qquad \mu=\frac1p,\quad \sigma=\frac{\sqrt{1-p}}p\]

To get the first success on trial k, fail k−1 times and then succeed. Independence lets us multiply those probabilities. Smaller p usually means longer waits and a longer right tail.

Worked example.

A player makes each free throw with probability 0.60, independently. First success on attempt 3 has probability \((0.40)^2(0.60)=0.096\). The expected wait is \(1/0.60\approx1.67\) attempts.

To wait more than k trials, all first k trials must fail: \(P(X>k)=(1-p)^k\). Some software counts failures before the first success instead; check the convention before using a calculator.

Check your understanding.

With p = 0.25, what is the expected trial number of the first success?

Show explanation

\(1/0.25=4\). This is an average over repetitions, not a promise of success on trial 4.

5. Binomial: count successes in a fixed number of trials

A binomial model fixes n in advance and counts successes. Trials are independent, each has two outcomes, and the success probability p remains constant. X can be 0, 1, …, n.

\[P(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad \binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\]

The factorial n! multiplies the integers from 1 through n, and 0! = 1. The combination counts arrangements: two heads in four flips can occur as HHTT, HTHT, HTTH, THHT, THTH, or TTHH.

Worked example.

For four independent fair coin flips, \(P(X=2)=6(0.5)^2(0.5)^2=0.375\). To find at least three heads, add \(P(X=3)+P(X=4)=4/16+1/16=0.3125\).

\[\mu=np,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}\]

The complement rule is often useful: the probability of at least one success is 1 minus the probability of no successes. With large n and p away from 0 and 1, binomial histograms become approximately bell-shaped after appropriate centering and scaling.

Check your understanding.

Inspect 20 items and count defects, or inspect until the first defect. Which model fits each?

Show explanation

The fixed group of 20 suggests a binomial model; waiting until the first defect suggests a geometric model. Both require independent trials with a constant defect probability.
