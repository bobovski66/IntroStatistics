Carry Week 1’s reasoning into a model

Last week we began with individual records and described their distribution, center, and spread. Now turn the direction around: before collecting observations, state what the process could produce and how much probability each result receives. The same ideas of weighted averages and deviations will describe that model.

Predict before calculating. Ask what one repetition is, what number is recorded, and what stays fixed. The geometric and binomial models will use the same success/failure trials while answering different questions.

  • Distinguish a given model probability from an observed relative frequency.
  • Build expected value and variance from probability weights.
  • Translate a verbal event into the outcomes being counted.

1. From observed frequencies to a model

A random variable assigns a numerical value to the result of a random process. For a spinner, the physical result is where the pointer lands; \(X\) can record the number printed there. Uppercase \(X\) names the variable; lowercase \(x\) names a possible numerical value.

A discrete probability distribution pairs each possible value with its probability. Its probability mass function, or PMF, gives \(P(X=x)\). The values can be counts, measurements on a discrete scale, or amounts such as net gain. They need not be equally likely, equally spaced, or nonnegative.

A model we can see.

An idealized spinner labels 20% of its angular range 0, 50% with 1, and 30% with 2. Assume the final pointer angle is uniform. Then the model probabilities are 0.20, 0.50, and 0.30.

Every probability must lie between 0 and 1, and the probabilities for all possible values must sum to 1. That total represents the whole set of possibilities. These are requirements for a model, rather than a pattern that an observed sample must match exactly.

A relative frequency is calculated from observations: count divided by the observed sample size. A model probability is a weight in our description of the process. Geometry can supply a probability in an idealized spinner; a real application may instead estimate probabilities from earlier data. In either case, assumptions connect the model to the actual process, and a new sample can disagree with its predictions.

An event collects outcomes that meet a condition. For the spinner, “at least one” includes 1 and 2. Since one spin cannot have both values, add their probabilities: \(P(X\ge1)=0.50+0.30=0.80\). The complement is \(X=0\), giving the same answer as \(1-0.20\).

In a discrete probability bar chart, heights are probability masses. Selected bars identify terms to add. Their graphical areas do not define the probabilities; the widths are a display choice. Next week’s continuous density curves will use area in a different way.

Check your understanding.

Can 0.2, 0.4, and 0.5 be the complete set of probabilities? If 10 simulated spins have relative frequencies different from the model, is the simulation necessarily wrong?

Show explanation

The proposed probabilities sum to 1.1, so they cannot be a complete PMF. A small simulated sample can differ from a valid model because of chance. Model validity and exact agreement with one sample are separate questions.

2. Build a probability-weighted mean

Week 1’s sample mean shares the observed total equally among cases. If values repeat, the same mean can be calculated by multiplying each distinct value by its observed relative frequency. To describe a given model, replace those observed fractions with the model’s probabilities.

\[\bar x=\sum_x x\frac{\text{observed count at }x}{n},\qquad \mu=E(X)=\sum_x xP(X=x).\]

Imagine, just to understand the arithmetic, a 100-record list containing exactly 20 zeros, 50 ones, and 30 twos. Its mean would be \((20\cdot0+50\cdot1+30\cdot2)/100=1.10\). The model’s expected value uses those same proportions. This imagined list explains the weighting; an actual set of 100 spins need not have exactly those counts.

Expected value is the model’s long-run average under independent repetition. It need not be a possible individual result, and it is not the same thing as the most likely result. The spinner’s most likely value is 1, while its expected value is 1.10.

Define gain before averaging it.

Suppose a game costs $2 per play and pays $10 only when a fair die shows 6. The random variable is net gain: subtract the fee in both cases.

ResultProbabilityGross payoutNet gain

\[E(\text{net gain})=8\left(\frac16\right)+(-2)\left(\frac56\right)=-\frac13\text{ dollar per play}.\]

The average loss is about 33 cents per play in the long run under this model. Neither individual net gain is −$0.33. A short run can still produce a profit or a much larger loss.

Check your understanding.

If every possible payout is increased by $1 while its probability stays fixed, how does the expected payout change?

Show explanation

It increases by \(\sum_x1\cdot P(X=x)=1\). Adding a constant to every outcome adds that same constant to the mean.

3. Weight the squared deviations

The model also needs a measure of spread. Use the same construction as Week 1: measure each outcome from its mean, square that deviation to prevent cancellation, and average the squared gaps. Now the averaging weights are probabilities.

\[\sigma^2=\sum_x(x-\mu)^2P(X=x),\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}.\]

For our spinner, the squared contributions are \((0-1.10)^2(0.20)=0.242\), \((1-1.10)^2(0.50)=0.005\), and \((2-1.10)^2(0.30)=0.243\). They total 0.49, so the theoretical standard deviation is \(\sigma=\sqrt{0.49}=0.70\) outcome units.

There is no \(n-1\) adjustment in this calculation. We are averaging over a fully specified probability distribution, rather than estimating an unknown population variance after fitting a sample mean. If we simulate observations and then calculate their sample standard deviation, that is a different calculation and the sample formula applies.

Another variance formula, derived from the same weights

Expand the square and use \(\sum_xP(X=x)=1\) and \(\sum_xxP(X=x)=\mu\):

\[\sigma^2=\sum_xx^2P(X=x)-2\mu\sum_xxP(X=x)+\mu^2\sum_xP(X=x)=E(X^2)-\mu^2.\]

Here \(E(X^2)=0^2(0.20)+1^2(0.50)+2^2(0.30)=1.70\), and \(1.70-1.10^2=0.49\). Keep \(E(X^2)\) separate from \([E(X)]^2\); squaring and averaging are different operations.

Check your understanding.

Two games have the same expected net gain but different standard deviations. What is the same, and what differs?

Show explanation

Their long-run average net gains agree. Their outcomes have different spread around that average. A common expectation does not make their individual results equally variable.

4. Geometric: wait for the first success

Suppose a process repeats trials with two classified outcomes: success and failure. “Success” means the outcome being counted; it could be an undesirable defect. Let the success probability be \(p\), constant on every trial, and assume the trials are independent. Then \(q=1-p\) is the failure probability.

The question determines what number we record. Let \(T\) count trials through and including the first success. Alternatively, we could stop after a fixed eight trials and count all successes as \(B\). Read each row below in both ways.

The geometric distribution models \(T\) under independent trials with constant \(0<p\le1\). Its possible values are 1, 2, 3, …. A first success on trial \(k\) requires a specific prefix: \(k-1\) failures, then one success.

\[P(T=k)=q^{\,k-1}p,\qquad k=1,2,3,\ldots.\]

For the free-throw teaching model with \(p=0.60\), first success on trial 3 has probability \((0.40)^2(0.60)=0.096\). The exponent counts failures before the successful trial. Software sometimes defines a geometric variable as the number of failures instead; check whether counting starts at 0 or 1.

To wait more than \(k\) trials, all first \(k\) must fail. To succeed by trial \(k\), take the complement:

\[P(T>k)=q^k,\qquad P(T\le k)=1-q^k.\]

The expected wait also follows from the trial structure. We always use the first trial. With probability \(q\), it fails and the independent, unchanged process begins again. Writing \(m=E(T)\) gives \(m=1+qm\), hence \(pm=1\) and \(m=1/p\). Applying the weighted squared-deviation rule to this distribution gives variance \(q/p^2\).

\[E(T)=\frac1p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac{1-p}{p^2},\qquad \operatorname{SD}(T)=\frac{\sqrt{1-p}}p.\]

At \(p=0.20\), the expected wait is 5 trials. That is an average across repetitions of the waiting experiment, rather than a deadline or a claim that success is most likely on trial 5. The most likely first-success trial is 1 when \(0<p<1\). At \(p=1\), the first trial succeeds with certainty.

Check your understanding.

Ten trials have all failed. Under the independent model with constant \(p=0.20\), is the next trial more likely to succeed because we have waited so long?

Show explanation

No. The next trial still has success probability 0.20. The observed prefix tells us \(T>10\), not that a success is “due.” This conclusion depends on the stated independent, constant-probability model.

5. Binomial: count successes in fixed trials

Now decide in advance to run \(n\) trials. Let \(B\) count successes among all of them. A binomial model requires that fixed number of trials, two classified outcomes on each trial, independence, and the same success probability \(p\) throughout. The possible counts are \(0,1,\ldots,n\).

To obtain two successes in four trials, the arrangement SSFF has probability \(p^2q^2\). But it is only one arrangement with that count. We must include all six:

The combination \(\binom nk\) counts choices of the \(k\) success positions. There are \(n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\) ways to choose and order those positions. Every unordered choice appears \(k!\) times, so divide by that number. The conventions \(0!=1\) and \(\binom n0=\binom nn=1\) account for the single all-failure or all-success arrangement.

\[\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!},\qquad P(B=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.\]

For four fair coin flips, exactly two heads has probability \(6(0.5)^2(0.5)^2=6/16=0.375\). To find an event containing several counts, add the corresponding bars.

Why is the mean \(np\)? Write the count as \(B=I_1+\cdots+I_n\), where \(I_j\) is 1 on a success and 0 on a failure. Each indicator has expected value \(1(p)+0(1-p)=p\). Adding expected contributions gives \(E(B)=p+\cdots+p=np\).

Derive the binomial spread from the same indicators

Because \(I_j^2=I_j\), its variance is \(E(I_j^2)-[E(I_j)]^2=p-p^2=p(1-p)\). Independent trials let their variances add, giving \(np(1-p)\). Taking a square root returns to success-count units.

Adding expectations does not itself require independence. The full binomial distribution and this variance formula do require the model’s independence assumption.

\[E(B)=np,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(B)=np(1-p),\qquad \operatorname{SD}(B)=\sqrt{np(1-p)}.\]

For the lab’s invented six-response model with \(p=0.70\), the expected count is 4.2. Individual groups still have whole-number counts. The standard deviation describes how counts vary across repeated groups under the model.

A binomial distribution can be skewed or symmetric. Its shape depends on both \(n\) and \(p\). When both expected successes \(np\) and expected failures \(n(1-p)\) are sufficiently large, a bell-shaped approximation becomes useful. Increasing \(n\) alone does not guarantee that a rare-success model is close to symmetric.

Check your understanding.

An inspector checks exactly 20 boards and counts defects, then in a separate experiment keeps checking until the first defect. Which variable is binomial and which is geometric? What needs checking before either model is used?

Show explanation

The fixed 20-board count suggests binomial; the trial number of the first defect suggests geometric. Both require independent classifications with a common defect probability. Sampling without replacement from a small batch can change that probability as the batch is depleted. The description of a task does not by itself establish every model assumption.

6. Connect the model with repeated observations

The law of large numbers connects a model’s expected value with averages of repeated observations. For independent repetitions of the finite-outcome models used here, the sample average converges to the expected value as the number of repetitions grows. The next step can still move away from that value. Individual outcomes keep their original variability.

In The Settling Average, choose a fair die. Record the average at 10, 100, and 1,000 rolls. Reset and repeat. The target stays 3.5 while the observed paths differ. One die roll can never be 3.5, and a long run of low rolls does not change the next roll’s probabilities under independence.

A useful model report states the trial, the variable and its units, the stopping rule, the probabilities, the assumptions, and the interpretation. The skywatch discussion makes that precision essential: a trial records whether a window contains at least one qualified event. A binomial count then counts positive windows, rather than the total number of lights.
