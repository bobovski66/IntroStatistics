One-Sample Hypothesis Tests

A hypothesis test asks whether a sample result is unusually far from a stated claim, measured against the variation that claim predicts.

How to use this reading

Read the explanations in order and pause at the worked examples. Try each check on paper before opening its explanation. Use the original PDF for more examples, exercises, and reference tables.

  • State null and alternative hypotheses before calculating.
  • Compute and interpret a test statistic and p-value.
  • Distinguish a decision from proof or practical importance.

1. Start with the claim and the direction of interest

The null hypothesis \(H_0\) specifies the value used to build a reference distribution. The alternative hypothesis \(H_a\) states the departure being investigated. In this course, null hypotheses are written as equality claims, such as \(\mu=8\) or \(p=0.85\).

QuestionAlternativeTest direction
Is the mean below 8?\(H_a:\mu<8\)Left-tailed
Is the mean above 8?\(H_a:\mu>8\)Right-tailed
Has the mean changed?\(H_a:\mu\ne8\)Two-tailed

Choose the alternative and significance level before examining the result. A two-tailed test counts unusually large departures in either direction; a one-tailed test counts departures in its specified direction.

Check your understanding.

A manufacturer claims a 500-hour mean battery life. A consumer group suspects shorter life. State the hypotheses.

Show explanation

\(H_0:\mu=500\) hours and \(H_a:\mu<500\) hours. This is a left-tailed test.

2. A test statistic measures departure in standard errors

A raw difference is hard to judge without a scale. Being 0.2 ounce below a claim is more striking when the sample mean usually varies by 0.02 ounce than when it varies by 0.5 ounce. The test statistic divides departure by the standard error under the null model.

\[\text{test statistic}=\frac{\text{observed statistic}-\text{null value}}{\text{standard error under }H_0}\]

For a one-sample mean z-test with known \(\sigma\), use \(z=(\bar x-\mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\). Check independent, representative sampling and a normal population or an adequate CLT approximation. A large n alone does not guarantee those conditions.

Worked example.

A machine should average 8.0 ounces. A random sample of 36 cups has \(\bar x=7.82\), with known \(\sigma=0.5\). Standard error is \(0.5/6=0.08333\); the statistic is \((7.82-8.0)/0.08333=-2.16\). The observed mean is 2.16 standard errors below the null mean.

When \(\sigma\) is estimated with s, the usual procedure is a one-sample t-test with \(t=(\bar x-\mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n)\) and n−1 degrees of freedom, rather than treating s as a known population quantity.

3. The p-value is conditional on the null model

A p-value is the probability, assuming the null model and its other conditions, of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one in the direction specified by the alternative. A small value indicates that the observed departure would be unusual under that reference model.

Test directionNormal-model p-value
Left-tailed\(\Phi(z_{\mathrm{obs}})\)
Right-tailed\(1-\Phi(z_{\mathrm{obs}})\)
Two-tailed\(2[1-\Phi(|z_{\mathrm{obs}}|)]\)
Worked example.

For the underfilling question, the p-value is \(\Phi(-2.16)\approx0.0154\). If the mean really were 8.0 ounces and the sampling model held, a sample mean this low or lower would occur about 1.54% of the time.

This is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability that chance “caused” the result. The null model is assumed to calculate the probability, rather than assigned a probability by the test.

Check your understanding.

For z = −2.16, would a right-tailed test have the same p-value?

Show explanation

No. Its p-value would be about 1 − 0.0154 = 0.9846, because its alternative looks for a higher mean.

4. Make a decision, then state what it means

The significance level \(\alpha\) is a chosen threshold, often 0.05. Reject \(H_0\) when the p-value is at most α; otherwise fail to reject it. For the coffee example, 0.0154 < 0.05, so there is evidence of underfilling at the 5% level.

Failing to reject does not prove the null true. A sample can provide too little information to detect a difference. Rejecting also does not establish a large or important effect. Report the observed departure and, where appropriate, a confidence interval along with the decision.

A Type I error rejects a true null hypothesis. A Type II error fails to reject when the relevant alternative is true. Under a valid test, α controls the Type I error rate; it does not give the probability that this particular rejection is mistaken.

Check your understanding.

A test gives p-value 0.032. What decisions follow at α = 0.05 and α = 0.01?

Show explanation

Reject at 0.05; fail to reject at 0.01. The data and p-value are unchanged, but the chosen evidence threshold differs.

5. One-sample proportion testing uses the null proportion

For \(H_0:p=p_0\), use the claimed proportion to calculate null sampling variation. Require at least 10 expected successes and 10 expected failures: \(np_0\ge10\) and \(n(1-p_0)\ge10\), with appropriate independent sampling.

\[z=\frac{\hat p-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}\]
Worked example.

A seed company claims p = 0.85. Of 200 seeds, 158 germinate: \(\hat p=0.79\). Expected null counts are 170 and 30. For \(H_a:p<0.85\), \(z\approx-2.38\), giving a left-tail p-value about 0.0087. Reject at α = 0.05.

In a confidence interval, use p-hat to estimate variability. In this test, use p₀ because the reference distribution assumes the null claim.

Connection to intervals.

For a known-σ mean procedure, a two-sided 5% z-test and its corresponding 95% z-interval agree: a null value strictly outside the interval is rejected. At an exact boundary the chosen equality convention matters. This equivalence requires matching procedures; the elementary proportion interval and test use different standard errors and need not agree exactly.
