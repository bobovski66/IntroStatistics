Continuous Distributions and z-Scores

For measurements on a continuous scale, probabilities are areas under a density curve. The standard normal model gives a common language for those areas.

1. Probability is area, not curve height

A continuous random variable models measurements such as time, length, or volume. A density curve is nonnegative and has total area 1. The probability of an interval is the area above that interval and below the curve.

\[P(a\le X\le b)=\text{area under the density from }a\text{ to }b\]

The density height is not the probability of the exact value below it. A narrow interval has probability approximately its width times the density height when that height changes little across the interval. A density can even exceed 1, provided its total area is 1.

For an absolutely continuous model, P(X = c) = 0 because one point has no width. This does not make observation impossible: measurement devices round values, and a recorded value usually represents a small interval. Including endpoints does not change continuous interval probabilities.

Check your understanding.

Does P(X = 2) = 0 mean that P(1.9 < X < 2.1) must also be zero?

Show explanation

No. The interval has positive width and can have positive area under the density.

2. Uniform: equal density over a fixed interval

A uniform distribution on [a, b] has constant density inside that interval and zero density outside it. Equal-length subintervals have equal probabilities.

\[f(x)=\frac1{b-a}\quad(a\le x\le b),\qquad P(c\le X\le d)=\frac{d-c}{b-a}\quad(a\le c\le d\le b)\]

The rectangle’s width is b−a and height is 1/(b−a), so its total area is 1.

Worked example.

A bus arrival time is modeled as uniform between 0 and 10 minutes. Arrival between 2 and 7 minutes has probability \((7-2)/10=0.50\). Arrival after minute 8 has probability \((10-8)/10=0.20\).

\[\mu=\frac{a+b}{2},\qquad \sigma=\frac{b-a}{\sqrt{12}}\]

For this bus model, the mean is 5 minutes and the standard deviation is about 2.89 minutes. A uniform model is an assumption about the process, not a consequence of merely knowing its minimum and maximum.

Check your understanding.

For a uniform model on [5, 20], what is P(8 ≤ X ≤ 15)?

Show explanation

The interval width is 7 and the total width is 15, so the probability is \(7/15\approx0.4667\).

3. The standard normal model and the empirical rule

The standard normal distribution is a symmetric bell curve with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. We use Z for a random variable with this distribution. Its total area is 1, and its tails extend in both directions.

\[f(z)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}\]

You will use a table or technology to find areas; evaluating the density formula does not give a cumulative probability. For a normal model, the empirical rule summarizes the central areas:

Interval around the meanApproximate proportion
Within 1 standard deviation68%
Within 2 standard deviations95%
Within 3 standard deviations99.7%

The 95% is the combined area between −2 and 2. The remaining 5% is split approximately equally between the two tails: about 2.5% in each. These percentages apply to normal or approximately normal shapes, not to every distribution.

Check your understanding.

Approximately what proportion of a normal distribution lies above 2 standard deviations from the mean?

Show explanation

About 2.5%, using the empirical rule. The more precise standard normal area is about 2.28%.

4. Use a left-tail table to answer three kinds of question

Define \(\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)\), the standard normal cumulative distribution function. The chapter’s z-table gives this left-tail area. For z = 1.23, use row 1.2 and column 0.03 to find 0.8907. Sketch the intended region before computing.

QuestionCalculation
Left of z\(\Phi(z)\)
Right of z\(1-\Phi(z)\)
Between a and b\(\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)\)
Worked example.

Left of 1.23: 0.8907. Right of 1.23: \(1-0.8907=0.1093\). Between −1.50 and 2.00: \(0.9772-0.0668=0.9104\).

Symmetry gives \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\). A negative cutoff lies left of the mean, so its left-tail area is less than 0.5. For a positive cutoff, the left-tail area exceeds 0.5.

Check your understanding.

The table gives Φ(1) = 0.8413. Find the area between −1 and 1.

Show explanation

\(0.8413-(1-0.8413)=0.6826\), agreeing with the approximate 68% rule.

5. Translate measurements into z-scores, or areas into cutoffs

A z-score measures distance from the mean in standard deviations: \(z=(x-\mu)/\sigma\). A positive score is above the mean, and a negative score is below it. Standardization can be useful for any distribution with positive finite standard deviation; using the normal table additionally requires a suitable normal model.

Worked example.

If an approximately normal measurement has mean 50 and standard deviation 10, then x = 65 has \(z=(65-50)/10=1.5\). Its left-tail probability is approximately 0.9332.

To work backward, find the z-value matching the desired left-tail area, then use \(x=\mu+z\sigma\). The 90th percentile of the standard normal is about 1.282. A cutoff with 10% above it has 90% below it.
