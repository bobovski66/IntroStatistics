Confidence Intervals

An estimate becomes more informative when we describe its uncertainty. A confidence interval attaches a margin of error to a sample statistic.

1. From a point estimate to an interval

A point estimate is one number: \(\bar x\) estimates a population mean \(\mu\), while \(\hat p=x/n\) estimates a population proportion p. A different sample usually gives a different estimate. An interval describes the uncertainty produced by that sampling variation.

\[\text{estimate}\ \pm\ \text{margin of error},\qquad ME=\text{critical value}\times SE\]

The margin of error is the distance from the estimate to either endpoint. It is not an absolute bound on all possible error. Its coverage depends on the chosen procedure and its conditions.

Check your understanding.

An interval is (42, 58). What are its center and margin of error?

Show explanation

The center is (42 + 58)/2 = 50; the margin of error is (58 − 42)/2 = 8.

2. What 95% confidence means

Imagine repeatedly selecting samples and constructing an interval from each one. The population parameter remains fixed, while the sample estimates and interval endpoints move. A valid 95% confidence procedure captures that fixed parameter in about 95% of its intervals over many repetitions.

For one realized interval, we report: “We are 95% confident that the population mean is between these endpoints.” The 95% describes the method’s long-run coverage. It is not the percentage of individual observations in the interval, nor a probability assigned to a particular parameter value after the calculation.

The actual fraction captured in a finite simulation can be above or below 95%. Approximate procedures can also depart from their nominal coverage when assumptions are poor. Increasing n addresses sampling variation; it does not automatically address a biased sample or measurement error.

Check your understanding.

A 95% interval for average commute time is 16 to 20 minutes. Does it contain 95% of student commute times?

Show explanation

No. It estimates the population mean, not the distribution of individual commute times.

3. A z-interval for a mean when σ is known

Try the confidence interval demo: draw repeated samples to build the stack, then compare intervals of different confidence levels from the same sample.

Use the chapter’s z-procedure when the population standard deviation is known and the observations are independent, with a normal population or a sufficiently accurate CLT approximation. Identify the population and check sampling conditions before calculating.

\[\bar x\pm z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]
Confidence levelCritical value z*Area in each tail
90%1.6450.05
95%1.9600.025
99%2.5760.005
Worked example.

A random sample of 40 adults has mean sodium intake 2,850 mg; assume known \(\sigma=400\) mg and an adequate normal approximation. Standard error is \(400/\sqrt{40}\approx63.25\) mg. At 95% confidence, \(ME=1.96(63.25)\approx124\) mg. The interval is approximately (2,726, 2,974) mg.

Report that the interval estimates the true mean intake for the population represented by the sample. The standard deviation σ describes individual variation; standard error describes how much a sample mean varies.

When σ is unknown.

A sample standard deviation s is not a known population standard deviation. The usual mean interval uses \(\bar x\pm t^*s/\sqrt n\), with n−1 degrees of freedom. The posted chapter develops known-σ z-intervals; keep that distinction when choosing a method.

4. An interval for a proportion

For a yes/no variable, count x successes among n observations. Use the estimated proportion in the standard error, because there is no specified null value for an estimation problem. The chapter’s normal interval requires at least 10 observed successes and 10 observed failures, together with appropriate independent sampling.

\[\hat p=\frac xn,\qquad SE=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n},\qquad \hat p\pm z^*SE\]
Worked example.

In a random sample of 320 students, 176 report daily breakfast. Then \(\hat p=176/320=0.55\), with 176 successes and 144 failures. Standard error is about 0.02781; the 95% margin is about 0.05451. The interval is approximately (0.4955, 0.6045), or 49.55% to 60.45%.

Keep proportions as decimals during computation. A margin of 0.0545 means 5.45 percentage points. Near proportions 0 or 1, or with small counts, this simple normal interval can perform poorly; a different interval method is needed.

Check your understanding.

Why use p-hat rather than an unknown p in this standard error?

Show explanation

The population proportion is what we are estimating. The sample proportion supplies an estimate of its sampling variability.

5. What changes interval width?

Holding the other quantities fixed, higher confidence gives a larger critical value and a wider interval. Greater population spread also widens a mean interval. Larger samples reduce standard error, narrowing the interval.

To halve the margin of error at a fixed confidence level and spread, quadruple the sample size.

To plan a known-σ mean interval with desired margin E, solve the margin formula for n and round up:

\[n\ge\left(\frac{z^*\sigma}{E}\right)^2\]
Worked example.

For 95% confidence, \(\sigma=3\) inches, and desired margin 0.5 inch, \(n\ge(1.96\cdot3/0.5)^2=138.2976\). Plan for at least 139 observations.

Finish every interval problem by naming the parameter, stating conditions, giving the interval with units, and interpreting it in context. A precise calculation needs an equally precise description of what it estimates.
