From Variation to Linear Prediction

Build the regression line from ideas we already know: the mean, deviations, squared error, and the uncertainty of an estimate.

  • Explain why the response mean is the best constant prediction under squared error.

  • Interpret predictions and r² using residuals, sampling variability, and the way the data were collected.

1. Bring the course’s questions together

In the first weeks, we described the center, spread, and shape of one variable. Sampling distributions then showed how a statistic changes across samples. Confidence intervals and hypothesis tests compared an estimate with the uncertainty around it. Two-sample methods asked whether a quantitative response or a yes/no outcome differed between groups. Categorical tables asked whether knowing one category changed the distribution of another.

Regression asks a related question when both variables are quantitative: does knowing x help us predict y? We still need an observational unit, clearly defined variables, a picture of the data, and a distinction between the records we have and the population we hope to describe.

A useful bridge from Week 7.

If x is an indicator coded 0 for one group and 1 for another, the least-squares line predicts the first group’s sample mean at 0 and the second group’s sample mean at 1. Its slope is their difference. A quantitative predictor lets us extend the idea of comparing responses across groups to a response that changes along a numerical scale. Fitting that descriptive line is separate from checking an inferential model.

2. Start with the prediction we already know: the mean

Suppose x has not been recorded. We must give the same predicted response c to every observation. One reasonable way to judge that prediction is to add its squared errors: \(\sum(y_i-c)^2\). This is the same kind of sum that appeared inside variance.

Why square? A signed sum would let an error of +3 cancel an error of −3, even though both predictions miss. Squaring keeps misses from cancelling and gives larger misses more weight. It is a choice of error measure; absolute errors would lead to a different optimization problem.

The mean is the constant that makes this sum smallest. To see why, write each error as \((y_i-\bar y)+(\bar y-c)\). The centered departures add to zero, so the cross term disappears:

\[\sum(y_i-c)^2=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2+n(c-\bar y)^2.\]

The first term is fixed by the data. The second is never negative and becomes zero exactly at \(c=\bar y\). Thus the mean is our best constant prediction under squared error. Regression will keep the same rule for judging errors while allowing different predictions at different x-values.

Pause and explain.

If a proposed constant is 2 response units above the mean in a dataset with 8 observations, how much larger is its total squared error?

Compare your reasoning

The extra error is \(n(c-\bar y)^2=8(2)^2=32\) response units squared. The mean is not simply a convenient horizontal reference; it wins this specific prediction contest.

Before fitting anything, describe direction, form, strength, and unusual points. Preserve the pairing: separately sorting x and y would manufacture a different dataset.

A predictor’s name describes its role in the model, not a causal conclusion. An observational preparation–score association could also reflect prior knowledge, the kind of preparation, or other variables. The sample’s picture and its data-collection process answer different questions.

Pause and explain.

Could a U-shaped scatterplot have a clear relationship and correlation near zero?

Compare your reasoning

Yes. Correlation summarizes a straight-line pattern. A strong curved association can have little net linear association. The scatterplot comes before the coefficient.

4. Turn a line into predictions and residuals

Let a proposed line predict \(\hat y=a+bx\). For the record with x = 4 and y = 5, the trial line \(\hat y=2+x\) predicts 6. Its residual is \(5-6=-1\): the observation is one score point below its prediction.

\[e_i=y_i-\hat y_i,\qquad SSE=\sum e_i^2.\]

Measure the errors vertically because this model predicts y from x. Reversing the roles of x and y changes the prediction task and usually changes the fitted line; correlation itself remains symmetric.

In the explorer, start with the horizontal prediction \(\hat y=7\). Tilt the line and watch the vertical residuals and their squared total. Moving one point nearer can move other points farther away. Least squares chooses the line with the smallest total squared error, not a line that passes through the most points.

Pause and explain.

Why is a signed total residual of zero insufficient evidence of a good fit?

Compare your reasoning

Large positive and negative misses can cancel. For these data the mean-only prediction has a zero signed sum but SSE = 60. We need the squared errors and a plot of their pattern.

5. Find the line’s anchor before its slope

First hold the slope b fixed. Imagine sliding a line of that slope up and down. Subtract the slope contribution from each response: \(y_i-bx_i\). Choosing an intercept is now the constant-prediction problem from Section 2, applied to those adjusted responses.

Their mean is \(\bar y-b\bar x\), so the best intercept for that slope is

\[a=\bar y-b\bar x.\]

Substitute \(x=\bar x\): the predicted value is \(\bar y\). Every least-squares line with an intercept therefore passes through the point of means \((\bar x,\bar y)\). In our eight records that point is (4.5, 7). We can now search for a slope by rotating the line around this anchor.

Why sliding the line gives this intercept

The same centering identity gives

\[\sum(y_i-a-bx_i)^2=\sum\big[(y_i-\bar y)-b(x_i-\bar x)\big]^2+n\big[\bar y-a-b\bar x\big]^2.\]

For a fixed slope, the first term does not depend on a. The last square is smallest at zero. No calculus is needed.

Pause and explain.

A fitted line has \(\bar x=6\), \(\bar y=40\), and slope 4.08. What intercept anchors it correctly?

Compare your reasoning

\(a=40-4.08(6)=15.52\). Substituting x = 6 into the line gives 40. This is the summary-statistics example in the lab.

6. Derive the slope from centered measurements

Centering removes the arbitrary origins of the axes. Write \(u_i=x_i-\bar x\) and \(v_i=y_i-\bar y\). A line through the anchor predicts the centered response as \(bu_i\), so each residual is \(v_i-bu_i\).

Two sums describe how x can help: \(S_{xx}=\sum u_i^2\), the spread available in x, and \(S_{xy}=\sum u_iv_i\), how x and y depart from their means together. Here is the calculation for the same eight records.

Expanding the squared residuals produces a quadratic in b:

\[SSE(b)=S_{yy}-2bS_{xy}+b^2S_{xx},\qquad S_{yy}=\sum v_i^2.\]

Complete the square to see its minimum:

\[SSE(b)=\left(S_{yy}-\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}}\right)+S_{xx}\left(b-\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}\right)^2.\]

As long as x varies, \(S_{xx}>0\). The second term is nonnegative and is zero at exactly one slope:

\[b=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac{48}{42}=\frac{8}{7},\qquad a=7-\frac{8}{7}(4.5)=\frac{13}{7}.\]

Thus \(\hat y=13/7+(8/7)x\). The slope is about 1.143 score points per preparation hour. At x = 4 it predicts \(45/7\approx6.429\), so that record’s residual is \(5-45/7=-10/7\approx-1.429\). Keep full precision until reporting the result.

What the derivation accomplished.

First choose the intercept that removes a common vertical offset. Then choose the slope that makes the remaining squared error smallest. The formulas describe that optimization; they are not an extra rule separate from the residual picture.

Pause and explain.

What fails if every observed x-value is the same?

Compare your reasoning

Then Sxx = 0. Many slope–intercept combinations make the same prediction at that one x-value; a unique slope cannot be identified. We can still predict the mean response at that x.

7. Connect the slope to z-scores and correlation

The centered cross-products still carry units. Standardizing each variable removes those units, using the z-score idea from earlier weeks:

\[r=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum\left(\frac{x_i-\bar x}{s_x}\right)\left(\frac{y_i-\bar y}{s_y}\right)=\frac{S_{xy}}{(n-1)s_xs_y}.\]

Pairs above both means or below both means contribute positively. Pairs on opposite sides contribute negatively. This gives the sign of linear association. The coefficient lies between −1 and 1, provided both standard deviations are positive.

We already know that \(S_{xx}=(n-1)s_x^2\). Substitute these identities into the slope we derived:

\[b=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}=\frac{(n-1)r\,s_xs_y}{(n-1)s_x^2}=r\frac{s_y}{s_x}.\]

This familiar formula is the same least-squares solution expressed through the summaries we know. A one-SD increase in x predicts an r-SD change in y. The slope restores the response-units-per-predictor-unit scale; r itself is unitless.

For our practice records, \(s_x=\sqrt{6}\), \(s_y=\sqrt{60/7}\), and \(r\approx0.9562\). The formula again gives \(b=8/7\). In standardized coordinates the prediction is simply \(\widehat z_y=r z_x\). For \(|r|<1\), the predicted standardized response is closer to its mean than the standardized predictor is to its mean. This is a property of the fitted association, not a claim that every individual changes in that direction.

Pause and explain.

Does converting preparation hours to minutes change correlation? What happens to the slope?

Compare your reasoning

Correlation stays the same because positive unit conversions do not change standardized values. The slope becomes 1/60 as large: it is now score points per minute.

8. Derive r² by comparing with the mean-only prediction

Return to our first prediction rule, \(\hat y=\bar y=7\). Its squared error is \(SST=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2=60\). The fitted line has \(SSE=36/7\approx5.143\). Knowing x has reduced the squared error by about 54.857 score points squared in these records.

For ordinary least squares with one predictor and an intercept, the fraction reduced is

\[R^2=1-\frac{SSE}{SST}=1-\frac{36/7}{60}=\frac{32}{35}\approx0.9143.\]

Why is this the square of correlation? The minimized-error expression in Section 6 gives \(SSE=S_{yy}-S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}\), while \(SST=S_{yy}\). Therefore

\[R^2=\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}}=r^2.\]

About 91.4% of the sample’s squared variation in score around its mean is accounted for by this linear fit. This does not mean 91.4% of predictions are exactly right or that preparation caused 91.4% of the score. The remaining variation can include noise, omitted variables, measurement error, or model mismatch; the residuals alone do not identify the cause.

If all responses are identical, the horizontal line fits them exactly. Correlation and R² are undefined in that case because the response SD and SST are zero.

The equality \(R^2=r^2\) uses this particular model: ordinary least squares, one predictor, and an intercept. A trial line you move by hand can do worse than the mean-only prediction, even though the optimized fit cannot have a larger SSE than that horizontal line.

Pause and explain.

If a fitted line reduces a mean-only squared error of 100 to 36, what is R²? Is that enough to compare its prediction accuracy with a model of an entirely different response variable?

Compare your reasoning

R² = 1 − 36/100 = 0.64. It measures proportional reduction for this response and dataset. It does not by itself compare absolute errors across unrelated responses or datasets.

9. Ask what the line misses

Plot the residuals against x. A line can summarize the overall trend while missing a curve, a change in spread, or one influential observation. A roughly patternless band around zero is more encouraging than a systematic pattern, but a small sample cannot certify that every model condition is satisfied.

A curved residual pattern points to a mean relationship the line has missed. A fan suggests that response variability changes across x; uncertainty for large x may differ from uncertainty for small x. A point with an x-value far from the others has high leverage. It becomes influential when its inclusion materially changes the fit. A point can help pull the line toward itself and then have only a modest fitted residual.

This is the Week 9 habit again: inspect the observation, fit with and without it as a sensitivity experiment, and explain the difference. Removal is not justified merely because a point is unusual. Check measurement and context before deciding whether it belongs in the primary analysis.

Predicting within the observed x-range is interpolation; beyond it is extrapolation. Being inside the range does not guarantee a precise or appropriate prediction. Even a good fitted line describes a typical response, while individual responses still vary around it.

Pause and explain.

What does a nearly zero residual for a far-right point tell you about its influence?

Compare your reasoning

Not enough. The point may have rotated the fitted line toward itself. Compare the fit with and without it, then consider whether the record is valid and belongs to the intended population.

10. Separate residual variation from sampling variation

The residual plot describes how observations in one sample miss that sample’s fitted line. Sampling variability is a different idea: if we repeated the data collection, how would the fitted line change? The slope and intercept are statistics, just as the mean and sample proportion were statistics in earlier weeks.

In the explorer’s sampling view, every sample is generated from the same stated model, \(Y=2+1.1x+\varepsilon\), with independent mean-zero normal errors. The true mean line stays fixed while new y-values produce different estimated lines. Try eight observations, collect several samples, then repeat with 32 at the same x-range and error spread. More information generally makes the fitted slopes vary less. Increasing the error spread generally makes them vary more.

This reconnects with the sampling-distribution and confidence-interval units. A fitted line is an estimate, not a population law, and r² from one sample does not quantify all uncertainty about it. Estimating a mean response and predicting a new individual response are different tasks: the new response also has its own unexplained variation.

Least-squares arithmetic needs variation in x; it does not require a normal distribution for x or y. Formal inferential procedures add assumptions about the conditional mean, independent errors, their spread, and their distribution. We are not adding a new slope-test formula here. The point is to recognize when a descriptive fit is being used to make a claim beyond the observed records.

Pause and explain.

If two samples give different fitted slopes, must one calculation be wrong?

Compare your reasoning

No. Sampling variation can change the fitted line even when both calculations are correct and the population model is unchanged. This is the same estimate-versus-parameter distinction used with sample means and proportions.

11. Finish with an argument from evidence

A regression report should be readable in the order the reasoning occurred: define the observations and variables; describe the scatterplot; give the fitted line and slope units; make a prediction in a defensible range; assess residuals and influence; interpret r²; and qualify the scope of the conclusion.

The course connection.

We began by describing variation. We learned how sampling makes estimates uncertain, how models let us compare observations with a hypothesis, and how definitions and influential records affect conclusions. Regression now uses a second quantitative variable to improve prediction while keeping all of those obligations. A formula is useful when we can explain what question it answers, why it takes that form, and what the data leave unresolved.

Optional: a precise connection to information

For a jointly Gaussian population pair with correlation \(\rho\) and \(|\rho|<1\), mutual information in bits is \(I(X;Y)=-\tfrac12\log_2(1-\rho^2)\). This describes a particular population model, not an identity obtained merely by inserting any sample correlation. Separate normal marginal distributions are not enough; joint Gaussian structure matters. Outside that model, zero correlation can coexist with substantial nonlinear dependence.

This extension connects prediction with reduction of uncertainty. The core regression calculations and this week’s guided lab do not require the information formula.
