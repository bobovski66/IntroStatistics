Linear Regression

A fitted line summarizes how two quantitative variables vary together. Its usefulness depends on the scatterplot, the prediction errors, and the range of observations.

How to use this reading

1. Begin with paired measurements and a scatterplot

Record two quantitative measurements from each observational unit. Plot the predictor x horizontally and response y vertically. Each point represents one pair (xᵢ, yᵢ). Calling x a predictor does not establish that it causes y.

Describe the direction of the pattern, its form (linear or curved), its strength, and unusual points. A numerical summary cannot replace this picture: a curve or one influential observation can make a straight-line summary misleading.

Check your understanding.

A plot of car age versus resale value trends downward. What is the direction?

Show explanation

Negative association: larger ages tend to accompany lower resale values. The plot is still needed to assess whether the form is approximately linear.

2. Correlation measures linear association

The correlation coefficient r is an average of products of standardized departures from the two sample means. It is defined when both variables have positive standard deviation.

\[r=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac{x_i-\bar x}{s_x}\right)\left(\frac{y_i-\bar y}{s_y}\right)\]

When both measurements are above their means, or both below, their product is positive. Opposite-signed departures contribute negatively. Correlation ranges from −1 to 1; the sign describes direction and proximity to ±1 describes strength of linear association.

Correlation has no units and is unchanged by swapping x and y. Converting inches to centimeters does not change it. A strong nonlinear association can have r near zero, so “no linear association” is more precise than “no relationship.”

Check your understanding.

Does r = −0.90 indicate a weaker linear pattern than r = 0.30?

Show explanation

No. The magnitude 0.90 indicates the stronger linear pattern. Its negative sign describes direction.

3. Least squares fits a line by prediction errors

Write the fitted prediction as \(\hat y=a+bx\). A residual is observed response minus predicted response: \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\). Positive residuals lie above the line; negative ones lie below it.

\[b=r\frac{s_y}{s_x},\qquad a=\bar y-b\bar x,\qquad \hat y=a+bx\]

The least-squares line minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals. With positive variation in x, the fitted slope and intercept are uniquely determined. The line passes through (x-bar, y-bar).

Worked example.

For eight adults, suppose daily sugar intake x (grams) and resting heart rate y (beats/minute) have \(\bar x=45\), \(s_x=12\), \(\bar y=74\), \(s_y=8\), and r = 0.72. The slope is \(0.72(8/12)=0.48\), and the intercept is \(74-0.48(45)=52.4\). Thus \(\hat y=52.4+0.48x\).

The slope predicts a 0.48 beats/minute increase in y for each additional gram in x within this fitted association. The intercept is the predicted value at x = 0; it may lack practical meaning when zero lies outside the observed range. This observational association does not establish that changing sugar intake causes the predicted change.

Check your understanding.

At x = 60, predict y. If the actual response is 78, find the residual.

Show explanation

\(\hat y=52.4+0.48(60)=81.2\). The residual is \(78-81.2=-3.2\) beats/minute.

4. Explained variation: what r² says

For ordinary least-squares regression with one predictor and an intercept, r² is the fraction of sample variation in y accounted for by the fitted line. Compare variation around the response mean with the remaining variation around the line.

\[SST=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2,\qquad SSE=\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2,\qquad r^2=1-\frac{SSE}{SST}\]
Worked example.

If r = 0.72, then r² = 0.5184. About 51.8% of the sample variation in resting heart rate is accounted for by this linear fit; about 48.2% remains in the residuals.

“Explained” here is an algebraic description of the fit, not proof of a causal explanation. It does not mean 51.8% of people receive correct predictions. Unexplained variation can reflect omitted factors, noise, or a poor choice of model.

Check your understanding.

For r = −0.80, compute r² and interpret its sign.

Show explanation

r² = 0.64, or 64% accounted for by the line. Squaring removes direction; retain r or the slope to describe the negative association.

5. Residual plots and the limits of prediction

Plot residuals against x or fitted values. A roughly patternless band around zero supports using a straight-line form. A curved pattern suggests that a line misses systematic structure. A fan-shaped spread suggests prediction uncertainty changes across x.

Inspect unusual and influential points in context; investigate possible errors rather than automatically deleting them. For inferential statements, independence of errors and additional model conditions also matter. A high r² alone does not establish a trustworthy model.

Predicting inside the observed x-range is interpolation. Predicting outside it is extrapolation and requires extra justification because the observed pattern may not continue. Even an in-range prediction for one person can be uncertain: the fitted line predicts a typical response, not an exact individual outcome.

Check your understanding.

The observed sugar-intake range is 20–80 grams. Is a prediction at 200 grams interpolation?

Show explanation

No. It is extrapolation, far outside the observed range. The data do not demonstrate that the fitted linear pattern persists there.

6. A connection to information, with its conditions

The PDF closes by relating regression to uncertainty and information. Learning x can make y easier to predict, but correlation captures only the linear part of an association.

For a jointly Gaussian pair of variables with population correlation \(\rho\) and \(|\rho|<1\), mutual information in bits satisfies:

\[I(X;Y)=-\frac12\log_2(1-\rho^2)\]

Joint normality is stronger than each variable having a normal marginal distribution. This identity is not a general formula for arbitrary data. Under the joint Gaussian model, zero correlation means independence; outside that model, zero correlation can coexist with substantial nonlinear dependence. As |ρ| approaches 1, the formula grows without bound in the ideal continuous model.

The course connection.

Descriptive statistics measured variation in one variable. Sampling distributions measured uncertainty in estimates. Regression now asks how much knowing a second variable can account for that variation, while retaining the same need to examine the data and the assumptions.
