Two-Sample Inference

Comparing groups requires a clear definition of the difference, a suitable study design, and a standard error that accounts for both samples.

How to use this reading

Read the explanations in order and pause at the worked examples. Try each check on paper before opening its explanation. Use the original PDF for more examples, exercises, and reference tables.

  • Distinguish independent and paired observations.
  • Test and estimate a difference in means.

1. Identify the design before selecting a formula

Two independent samples involve distinct groups with no pairing between their observations. Paired observations have a natural match: the same people before and after treatment, or matched individuals. Equal sample sizes alone do not make samples paired.

For paired measurements, the change within each pair is the relevant observation. Analyze those changes with a one-sample method when its conditions hold. An independent-samples formula would ignore their dependence. The posted chapter develops independent-sample procedures.

Check your understanding.

Two classes each contain 30 students. Are their scores necessarily paired?

Show explanation

No. Pairing requires a meaningful correspondence between observations, not merely equal group sizes.

2. Define the difference and keep its order

Label the groups before calculating. The population parameter is \(\mu_1-\mu_2\), estimated by \(\bar x_1-\bar x_2\). A positive difference means Group 1 has the larger mean; reversing the order reverses the sign. A claim of equal means is \(H_0:\mu_1-\mu_2=0\).

For independent sample means, their variances add when taking a difference. Both samples contribute uncertainty; their standard deviations are not simply added or averaged.

\[SE=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}\]

The chapter uses known population standard deviations. Check independent sampling within and between groups and a suitable normal approximation for both means. Strong skewness can make the approximation poor despite apparently large samples.

Check your understanding.

If both sample means estimate equal population means, where is their difference distribution centered?

Show explanation

At zero. Observed differences still vary from sample to sample.

3. A two-sample z-test for means

Use the same hypothesis-testing steps as last week: state the hypotheses and α, check conditions, compute the statistic, find the appropriate tail probability, and conclude in context.

\[z=\frac{(\bar x_1-\bar x_2)-0}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1+\sigma_2^2/n_2}}\]
Worked example.

Two teaching-method groups have \(n_1=35\), \(\bar x_1=81.4\), known \(\sigma_1=9.2\); and \(n_2=40\), \(\bar x_2=76.8\), known \(\sigma_2=11.0\). The difference is 4.6 points. Standard error is \(\sqrt{9.2^2/35+11^2/40}\approx2.333\), so \(z\approx1.97\). The two-sided p-value is about 0.0488, using the rounded z-table value.

At α = 0.05, reject equality under the stated model. This is a result close to the threshold, so include the estimated difference and its uncertainty. A causal claim about the teaching methods also needs a suitable experimental design.

When the standard deviations are estimated.

The usual independent-samples mean procedure uses Welch’s t-method with \(SE=\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}\) and an appropriate degrees-of-freedom calculation. A large-sample z approximation using these sample SDs is an approximation, not the known-σ procedure.

4. An interval adds magnitude and direction

For the same known-σ setting, the confidence interval estimates the population difference. Keep the units and group order in the interpretation.

\[(\bar x_1-\bar x_2)\pm z^*\sqrt{\sigma_1^2/n_1+\sigma_2^2/n_2}\]
Worked example.

The teaching-method 95% interval is \(4.6\pm1.96(2.333)\), approximately (0.03, 9.17) points for Method 1 minus Method 2.

This interval excludes zero but includes effects ranging from almost negligible to several points. A statistical decision alone does not describe that range. An interval crossing zero is consistent with differences in either direction; it does not establish exact equality.

Check your understanding.

An interval for μ₁ − μ₂ is (−3.2, 8.6). Which direction is supported decisively?

Show explanation

Neither direction is established by this interval, because it includes zero and both signs. Its midpoint is 2.7, indicating the sample difference is positive.

5. Two-sample proportion testing: why pool?

Now the response is binary in each group. Compute \(\hat p_1=x_1/n_1\) and \(\hat p_2=x_2/n_2\). Under \(H_0:p_1=p_2\), estimate the common proportion from the combined counts:

\[\hat p_{\mathrm{pool}}=\frac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2},\qquad z=\frac{\hat p_1-\hat p_2}{\sqrt{\hat p_{\mathrm{pool}}(1-\hat p_{\mathrm{pool}})(1/n_1+1/n_2)}}\]

Pooling uses total successes divided by total observations, not an unweighted average of percentages from unequal groups. Check the expected success and failure counts in each group under the pooled null; use at least 10 of each for the normal approximation. The chapter also asks you to inspect the four observed counts.

Worked example.

Group 1 has 18 of 120 with a side effect; Group 2 has 31 of 110. Proportions are 0.1500 and about 0.2818. The pooled estimate is \(49/230\approx0.2130\). The null standard error is about 0.05405, so z is about −2.44. A left-tailed alternative gives p-value about 0.0074.

For an interval estimating \(p_1-p_2\), use an unpooled standard error \(\sqrt{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)/n_1+\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)/n_2}\). Equality is assumed for the pooled test, not for estimating the difference.

Check your understanding.

Group 1 has 10 successes in 20 observations and Group 2 has 90 in 100. Find the pooled estimate.

Show explanation

\((10+90)/(20+100)=100/120\approx0.8333\), not the average of 0.50 and 0.90.
