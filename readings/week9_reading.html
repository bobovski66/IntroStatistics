Applied Inference: The Q1 2026 Fireball Data

The same observations can answer different questions. This investigation compares averages and threshold proportions, then asks how strongly the conclusions depend on the data and the model.

1. Define exactly what is being compared

The weekly lab contains 261 event records: 194 first-quarter events from 2021–2025 and 67 first-quarter events from 2026. Each row is one event. The numerical variable reports_count is the number of eyewitness reports submitted for that event.

An event with 200 reports is one observation with value 200, not 200 separate events. A witness report count is also not a direct measurement of a meteor’s mass, energy, or brightness. Visibility, population density, timing, weather, and reporting practices can affect it.

Keep the comparison precise: first-quarter 2026 events versus the pooled first-quarter 2021–2025 baseline in the supplied data. Those records are not automatically a random sample of every meteor occurring worldwide. Inference beyond the records depends on whether the sampling and reporting process supports it.

Source reading.

The course links to the American Meteor Society’s Q1 2026 analysis. If that article is temporarily unavailable, use the dataset, source context, and tables retained in the weekly lab and Fireball Case Files. Distinguish the supplied records from broader source claims.

Check your understanding.

What does a row with reports_count = 51 represent?

Show explanation

One event with 51 submitted eyewitness reports. It does not represent 51 independent meteor events.

2. Describe the groups before making a test

Compute sample size, mean, median, standard deviation, and maximum for each group. Make histograms using comparable bin boundaries. Check whether most observations cluster at low report counts while a few extend far to the right.

A large value can affect the mean and standard deviation at the same time. The lab’s 2026 data include a 3,229-report event. Its size warrants inspection and context; being extreme does not by itself make the record incorrect or justify deleting it.

Worked example.

To see the mechanism with a small teaching example, compare 30, 40, 50, 60 with 30, 40, 50, 600. The means are 45 and 180, while both medians are 45. The second set is much more spread out. These four-value lists illustrate sensitivity; they are not the fireball dataset.

Check your understanding.

Does a higher sample mean imply that most events have more reports?

Show explanation

No. A small number of very large values can raise the mean even if most observations are similar. Compare medians and graphs as well.

3. The difference of means depends on its standard error

Keep the order as 2026 minus baseline. The estimate is \(\bar x_{2026}-\bar x_{\mathrm{base}}\). The weekly lab estimates sampling variability using sample standard deviations:

\[\widehat{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{s_{2026}^2}{67}+\frac{s_{\mathrm{base}}^2}{194}}\]

The lab applies a large-sample z approximation: divide the observed difference by this estimated standard error, then use normal tail areas. For its 95% interval, multiply the same standard error by 1.96. These are the assigned calculations, but their reliability must be evaluated rather than assumed.

When population SDs are unknown, Welch’s t-method is the usual independent-sample mean procedure. Neither a t-reference nor a z-reference automatically resolves severe skewness, influential extremes, dependence among events, or selection through reporting. The lab’s diagnosis asks you to see whether the tools adequately address the question.

Holding the mean difference fixed, a larger standard error reduces the magnitude of the statistic and increases the two-sided p-value. An extreme event can increase both the difference and its estimated uncertainty. The resulting decision depends on their ratio.

Check your understanding.

Can the sample means differ visibly while a test fails to reject equal population means?

Show explanation

Yes. If the estimated sampling variability is large relative to the difference, the statistic may not be sufficiently extreme. Failure to reject does not establish equal means.

4. A threshold changes the question

Define a “large-report” event by a specified cutoff, such as reports_count ≥ 50. Each event now becomes a yes/no observation. The new parameter is the proportion exceeding that threshold, rather than the mean report count.

An event with 51 reports and an event with 3,229 reports each contribute one success. Thresholding limits the influence of the magnitude of extreme values, but discards information about how far above the cutoff they lie.

\[\hat p_{2026}=\frac{x_{2026}}{67},\quad \hat p_{\mathrm{base}}=\frac{x_{\mathrm{base}}}{194},\quad \hat p_{\mathrm{pool}}=\frac{x_{2026}+x_{\mathrm{base}}}{261}\]

Use the pooled null standard error from Week 7 for the test of equal proportions. Check expected successes and failures in both groups under the pooled null. The required cutoff is 50; the slider then explores how the result changes at other cutoffs.

Check your understanding.

Does “more events exceed 50 reports” mean the same thing as “the mean report count is larger”?

Show explanation

No. One describes a threshold probability; the other describes an average magnitude. They are different population features, so their tests can lead to different decisions.

5. Sensitivity is part of the conclusion

Compare the descriptive shares at the lab’s cutoffs 25, 50, 100, and 150. At 25 every row qualifies, so a p-value is not defined. At each other cutoff check the four pooled expected counts before using the normal approximation; if any is below 10, keep the comparison descriptive. Stability across defensible cutoffs can support a broader description; changing conclusions show how much the result depends on the chosen definition.

Exploring several thresholds helps understand the data. Selecting a cutoff afterward solely because it gives the smallest p-value undermines the interpretation of that p-value as a single preplanned test. Report the explored thresholds and distinguish exploration from a confirmed finding.

The mean and proportion analyses need not contradict each other: their questions and sensitivities differ. Your report should name the observational unit, summarize both groups, state each parameter and hypothesis, give the estimates and uncertainty, and explain which conclusions the data support under the assumed models.

Write a careful synthesis.

Explain how the 3,229-report event affects the mean and spread; explain what thresholding keeps and discards; discuss whether conclusions depend on the cutoff; and distinguish changes in these recorded witness counts from claims about meteor physics or causes. Use your calculated results rather than assuming a particular decision.

6. Connect a region to categorical evidence

Case File 5 returns to Week 8. Define one broad sky zone and classify every selected event as inside or outside it. Cross those two categories with baseline versus 2026 to form a two-by-two table. Under independence, use the row and column totals to calculate each expected count, then add the four Pearson chi-square contributions.

Check expected counts of at least 5, use one degree of freedom, and interpret a right-tail probability. This tests association in zone membership under the model; it does not establish a meteor source or physical cause. The tight zone is contained within the broad zone, so the zone names cannot be columns of a single table of exclusive categories.

Try it with records.

In the sky view, inspect an event just inside or just outside a boundary. How could uncertainty in the estimated direction affect its classification? Compare counts with percentages before interpreting a cluster.
