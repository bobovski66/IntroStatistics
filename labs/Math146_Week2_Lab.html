Problem 1 — Probability Distribution and Expected Value

Goal: Check a discrete probability model and use it to compute an expected value.

A simple spinner has four possible outcomes. It lands on 1 with probability 0.10, on 2 with probability 0.30, on 3 with probability 0.40, and on 4 with probability 0.20. This deliberately simple model makes the rules visible: probabilities must add to 1, and the expected value is a probability-weighted average rather than the outcome of any one spin.
(a & b) Complete the table below and verify the sum equals 1.
\(x\)1234Sum
\(P(X=x)\)0.10 0.40
Compare with a worked solution

\(P(X=2)=0.30\), \(P(X=4)=0.20\); the four probabilities sum to \(1.00\).

Every probability distribution's values must total exactly 1. Since \(0.10+0.40=0.50\) is already assigned, the remaining two probabilities must sum to \(0.50\) as well; here they split as \(0.30\) and \(0.20\).

(c) Sketch the probability histogram: what are the bar heights, and what do the axes represent?
Compare with a worked solution

Four bars at \(x=1,2,3,4\) with heights \(0.10,0.30,0.40,0.20\).

The horizontal axis lists the possible spinner values; the vertical axis is probability. Each bar's height equals \(P(X=x)\). The heights add to one; bar widths are chosen for legibility and do not define probability in this discrete graph.

(d) Compute \(\mu=E(X)\).
Compare with a worked solution

\(E(X)=2.70\).

\(E(X)=1(0.10)+2(0.30)+3(0.40)+4(0.20)=0.10+0.60+1.20+0.80=2.70\). Across many independent spins, the mean of the recorded values approaches 2.7. A single spin still produces only 1, 2, 3, or 4.

(e) Carry Week 1’s deviations into the probability model. Use μ = 2.7 and calculate the four contributions (x − μ)²P(X = x). Find the theoretical variance and standard deviation. Keep the original spinner probabilities.

Compare with a worked solution

The contributions are 0.289, 0.147, 0.036, and 0.338. They total σ² = 0.81 spinner units², so σ = 0.90 spinner units. We are averaging with the given model probabilities, rather than estimating an unknown population variance from a fitted sample mean.

Problem 2 — Geometric Distribution

Goal: Recognize a geometric setting and compute probabilities and its expected waiting time.

A student answers a sequence of independent five-choice practice questions by guessing, so each question has probability \(p=0.20\) of being correct. Let \(X\) be the question number of the first correct answer. Because the number of trials is not fixed in advance—we keep going until the first success—this is a geometric setting.
(a) Compute \(P(X=3)\) using \((1-p)^{k-1}\cdot p\) with \(k=3\).
Compare with a worked solution

\(P(X=3)=0.128\).

\(P(X=3)=(0.80)^2\times0.20=0.64\times0.20=0.128\). Two wrong guesses must happen first, then a correct one on the third try.

(b) Compute \(\mu=1/p\) and interpret it in one sentence.
Compare with a worked solution

\(\mu=1/0.20=5\).

On average, a student guessing with a 20% success rate needs 5 questions before getting the first one right.

(c) Describe the histogram shape: symmetric, skewed left, or skewed right? Why?
Compare with a worked solution

Skewed right, with the tallest bar at \(k=1\).

Geometric probabilities \((1-p)^{k-1}p\) are largest at \(k=1\) and shrink every time \(k\) increases, since each extra failure multiplies by another factor of \(1-p<1\). That produces a tall bar near \(k=1\) and a long thin tail stretching to the right.

After Problem 2: If the first ten guesses are wrong, what can you say about T? What is the probability of a correct answer on the next guess under this model? Explain why a finite display must retain a tail category.

Problem 3 — Binomial Distribution

Goal: Recognize a binomial setting and compute probabilities, mean, and standard deviation.

In an invented teaching model, a treatment has response probability 0.70 for each patient. Six patients receive the treatment, and \(X\) records how many respond. The binomial model treats the six responses as a fixed number of independent success/failure trials with the same success probability \(p=0.70\). The calculation is useful only to the extent that those assumptions are reasonable.
(a) Identify \(n\) and \(p\); list the four binomial conditions and distinguish the features specified by the story from the assumptions adopted by the model.
Compare with a worked solution

\(n=6\), \(p=0.70\). A fixed six trials and binary response rule are specified. Independence and a common probability are model assumptions.

A binomial setting needs: (1) a fixed number of trials, here \(n=6\) patients; (2) independent trial outcomes, assumed here rather than established by the stated response rate; (3) only two outcomes per trial, effective or not; (4) the same probability of success on every trial, \(p=0.70\), also assumed here. Shared conditions or differences among patients could make those assumptions inappropriate in a real study.

(b) Compute \(P(X=6)\).
Compare with a worked solution

\(P(X=6)\approx0.1176\).

\(\binom{6}{6}=1\), so \(P(X=6)=(0.70)^6=0.117649\approx0.1176\). This is the model probability of six responses in one six-patient group; it is not evidence about an actual treatment.

(c) Compute \(P(X=4)\); show the combination \(\binom{6}{4}\) first.
Compare with a worked solution

\(\binom{6}{4}=15\); \(P(X=4)=15(0.2401)(0.09)=0.3241\).

Computing the probabilities for all seven counts confirms that four responses is the most likely count for this model. The expected value 4.2 is a different summary from that most likely count.

(d) Compute \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and interpret \(\mu\).
Compare with a worked solution

\(\mu=6(0.70)=4.2\); \(\sigma=\sqrt{6(0.70)(0.30)}=\sqrt{1.26}\approx1.12\).

Across many groups of 6 patients, we'd expect an average of 4.2 to respond to the drug, typically varying by about 1.12 patients from that average.

(e) Describe the expected histogram shape: symmetric, skewed left, or skewed right? Where is the tallest bar? Why?
Compare with a worked solution

Skewed left, with the tallest bar near \(x=4\).

Since \(p=0.70>0.50\), successes are more common than failures, which pulls most of the outcomes toward the higher end of \(0\) through \(6\) and stretches a shorter tail toward 0. The full probability table confirms that the tallest bar is at \(x=4\). The mean \(\mu=4.2\) summarizes repeated groups rather than selecting a possible count.
