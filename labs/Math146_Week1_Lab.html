What you’ll practice

  • Distinguish a population from a sample and match each to the correct symbol (\(\mu\) vs. \(\bar{x}\), \(\sigma\) vs. \(s\)).
  • Evaluate summation expressions such as \(\sum x_i\) and \(\sum x_i^2\) for a small data set.
  • Build a frequency and relative-frequency table and read percentages from it.
  • Compute a sample mean and sample standard deviation by hand, and predict how an outlier changes each.
Lab question: How can a small set of observations be used to describe a larger group without confusing a sample with the population it represents?

Why this matters: Nearly every inferential method later in the course begins here. A sample gives us statistics we can calculate; the population has parameters we usually cannot observe directly. Descriptive statistics are the first attempt to summarize what the sample is telling us.

Problem 1 — Population, Sample, and Notation

Goal: Identify the population, the sample, and the notation that belongs to each.

A county health department wants to learn about sleep habits among adults in the county. Measuring every adult is unrealistic, so 120 adults are randomly selected and asked how many hours they slept last night. Their mean is 6.8 hours. The sample is the group actually measured; the population is the larger group the department wants to understand. Use this setting to keep those two ideas—and their notation—separate.
(a) Identify the population.
Compare with a worked solution

The population is all adults in the county.

The population is the entire group the health department wants to learn about, whether or not every member was actually measured.

(b) Identify the sample.
Compare with a worked solution

The sample is the 120 adults who were randomly selected and measured.

The sample is the subset actually observed; it is used to estimate facts about the population.

(c) Which symbol, \(\mu\) or \(\bar{x}\), represents 6.8 hours? Explain.
Compare with a worked solution

\(\bar{x}\), the sample mean.

6.8 hours was computed from the sample of 120 adults, not from every adult in the county, so it is a statistic (\(\bar{x}\)) rather than a parameter (\(\mu\)).

(d) Which Greek letter represents the unknown true average?
Compare with a worked solution

\(\mu\), the population mean.

\(\mu\) is the true average sleep time across every adult in the county. It is unknown and unobservable directly; \(\bar{x}\) is our best estimate of it.

Problem 2 — Summation Notation and Frequency

Goal: Translate a small dataset into summations, frequencies, relative frequencies, and percentages.

Ten students at a campus café report how many cups of coffee they drank yesterday: 0, 1, 2, 1, 3, 0, 2, 1, 0, 2. Before a dataset can be modeled, it has to be organized. Use these ten observations to translate raw data into summation notation, frequencies, relative frequencies, and percentages.
(a) Compute \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{10} x_i\) (add all ten values).
(b) Compute \(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{10} x_i^2\) (square each value first, then add).

(d) What percentage of students drank at least one cup?
Compare with a worked solution

\(\sum x_i=12\), \(\sum x_i^2=24\), frequencies 3/3/3/1, and 70% drank at least one cup.

\(\sum x_i = 0+1+2+1+3+0+2+1+0+2=12\), and squaring first gives \(\sum x_i^2=0+1+4+1+9+0+4+1+0+4=24\). Cups \(0,1,2,3\) occur \(3,3,3,1\) times out of 10, so the relative frequencies are \(0.30,0.30,0.30,0.10\), summing to 1.00. Seven of the ten students drank at least one cup (everyone except the three who drank 0), so \(7/10=70\%\).

Problem 3 — Mean and Standard Deviation

Goal: Compute and interpret the sample mean and sample standard deviation, including the effect of an outlier.

Five friends report how many text messages they sent yesterday: 12, 25, 8, 31, 14. The dataset is deliberately small so every deviation from the mean can be seen. Compute the sample mean and sample standard deviation, then investigate what happens when one unusually large observation is added.
(a) Compute the sample mean \(\bar{x}\).
(b) Complete the deviation table and compute the sample standard deviation \(s\).

Compare with a worked solution

\(\bar{x}=90/5=18\); deviations \(-6,7,-10,13,-4\) (sum \(0\)); squared deviations \(36,49,100,169,16\) (sum \(370\)); \(s=\sqrt{370/4}=\sqrt{92.5}\approx9.62\).

The deviations always sum to zero — that is a built-in check. Dividing the sum of squared deviations by \(n-1=4\) and taking the square root gives the sample standard deviation, \(s\approx9.62\).

(c) A sixth friend sent 500 messages. Without computing, what happens to \(\bar{x}\) and \(s\)?
Compare with a worked solution

Both \(\bar{x}\) and \(s\) increase sharply.

500 is far above the other five values, so it pulls the mean upward substantially. It also sits very far from the new mean, so its squared deviation dominates the sum, making \(s\) much larger too. A single extreme value can distort both statistics — this is why the mean and standard deviation are called non-resistant measures.
